# Lecture notes: signal and noise

These notes accompany the stagekit deck "signal and noise" and explain its contents for reading afterwards, in the order of the slides. Build-up steps and the segments of an animation count as frames of their own in the player and in the export (67 frames in all), so the references point to frames. The deck was put together on 2026-11-08 from the decks "why 16 and not 4?" and "measuring and experimenting".

The core message: how many symbols a link carries is not decided by its code but by the ratio of the distance between the symbols to the scatter of the readings. Every measure that improves this ratio has a price, and whether it works on your own setup is shown only by a clean measurement series.

The running example is a colour sensor that reads the light of an LED, as in a link that transmits data with light. Any other sensor behaves the same in principle.

## Part 1: what the numbers say

**The lighthouse in the fog (Frame 4).** The lamp burns exactly as brightly as on a clear night, and yet the fog swallows the beam after a few metres. The signal has not become weaker; the disturbance has become stronger. Every LiFi link is in the same position: all senders are equally bright and all sensors equally good, and still the teams get very different distances.

**How many colours does your alphabet have? (Frame 5).** The question goes to every team, together with what each team did about noise. The answers differ, although every team works with the same LED, the same sensor and the same task, and that sets the direction of the deck: every team did something different and got a different result. To find out where the difference comes from, we first look at what arrives at the sensor at all.

**Four numbers instead of a colour (Frames 6 to 8).** The colour sensor delivers four values. Three channels measure through red, green and blue filters, and the fourth, called clear, measures all the incoming light without a filter, which makes it especially sensitive to bright and dark. The four numbers `r`, `g`, `b` and `c` are the program's actual input, and only processing turns them into the output `"red"`, `"blue"` or another symbol name. A sensor does not deliver the meaning of what it measures.

**Sent is not measured (Frames 9 to 11).** If you set the LED to `r=255, g=0, b=0`, it is easy to expect the same numbers at the sensor. On the slide the light travels across, and the four values count up at the sensor to something like `r=203, g=41, b=57, c=310`. That is not a defect but the normal case: the colour filters overlap, light gets lost with distance and angle, and room light reaches the sensor as well. A formula that calculates today's readings back to the LED values would already be wrong at the next table or on the next day, so we work with the readings of our own setup; part 2 shows how.

**Measure the dark first (Frames 12 to 15).** Even with its own LED switched off, the sensor does not show zero, because room light is always there. That is why every measurement series begins with a control reading with the LED switched off, the baseline against which all later values are read. A reading is made up of ambient light, the light of your own LED and noise, and the control reading estimates the first of them. It also tells you when the surroundings change during a series, for instance through afternoon sun or the LED of a neighbouring team.

**Ten readings of one colour (Frames 16 to 19).** The LED shows the same red ten times and nothing changes, yet the sensor reports ten different numbers. The first is there from the start, and the other nine arrive one after the other. That surprises most people, because a computer is the one machine that reliably gives the same output for the same input. But a sensor never gets the same input twice: the light flickers, the electronics hiss, someone walks past the window. This variation is called **noise**, and every measuring instrument has it; ten identical values would be more suspicious than ten different ones.

Then the same ten values land on a number line, and you see what is left of them: a spread from 207 to 220. Last, the mean appears as a blue line, x̄ = 213.5. The mean describes where the series lies and the spread how far it wanders, and two series can have the same mean and very different spreads, so both belong in the record. From this follows something that carries the whole deck: **a single reading says almost nothing.** The value 220 could be this colour, or a neighbouring colour in a bad moment, and only the ten together show where the colour really sits and how far it wanders.

**Five readings, one mean (Frames 20 to 23).** Twenty readings of the same colour stand in the upper row. Five of them turn yellow, travel down and meet at their mean, where a single dot stays; the first group goes slowly, the other three faster. In the end the lower row holds four means instead of twenty readings, and the brackets compare: the single readings reach from 207 to 220, the means only from 212.4 to 215.2. **A mean of five scatters less than a single reading, but you get one value instead of five**, and part 2 shows the same bargain on a whole signal.

**The scatter decides (Frame 24).** This is the answer to the question from the beginning, set early, with the evidence to follow: how many colours an alphabet can have is decided by the scatter of the readings. The code has to take the scatter into account sensibly, for instance when calibrating and deciding, but it cannot get more colours out of a link than its scatter allows. Noise is not a defect and not a bug but a property of every measurement, and whoever makes it smaller fits in more colours.

## Part 2: distance against spread

**The same signal, averaged over longer windows (Frames 26 to 30).** There is a way to make the numbers calmer: measure for longer and average what arrives. At the top is the raw signal as it reaches the sensor, far too restless for a decision in a single instant. Below it the same signal is averaged, first over 4, then over 12, then over 32 readings. In each strip a yellow line runs through the same stretch of time, and a mean appears as soon as its window is full, so the bars settle closer and closer to the true level.

The place to look is the right-hand edge, where the bars are counted: the same stretch of time holds 288 raw readings, then 72, 24 and 9 means, and only after the last strip has filled does the punchline appear. **Calmness is not free, you pay for it in readings per second**, and readings per second are exactly what your speed is made of. The spread also falls only with the square root of the window, so four times the window merely halves it. The sensor makes the same bargain with its integration time, the reading window over which it collects light. Gain is the second knob: it scales signal and noise alike and can drive the sensor into saturation.

**Try it: the noisy sensor (Frame 31).** The slide links the demonstrator [[noisy-sensor]]. All three of its lanes read the same sensor at the same moment: the top one decides from a single reading, the two below from the mean of five and of ten, and a red dot is a wrong decision. Send 25 symbols and compare the errors, then turn the noise up and see which lane still gets every symbol right. The figure below the errors is the price, because ten readings per symbol leave only two symbols per second.

**Two symbols, two clouds (Frames 32 to 34).** Now two symbols on the same scale, each not a single value but a cloud of values whose width is the noise from before. The small yellow bracket `s` below the right cloud marks one standard deviation, the usual measure of that width. At first the clouds are wide, and the parts that reach across the boundary, drawn red, are exactly the readings the receiver assigns to the wrong symbol. Then the clouds narrow, as they do with a longer window or shielded light, and at the same distance `d` every reading lands on its own side. Neither the distance nor the width decides on its own.

**The ratio is what counts (Frames 35 to 37).** The clouds stay exactly where they were on the slide before, and only the texts change: d becomes the distance between the means, s one standard deviation, and d/s appears on the left. The quantity this deck is about is this quotient. It counts how many standard deviations fit between the two means, so it has no unit and does not depend on whether the sensor counts to 255 or to 65,535. Each cloud reaches about three standard deviations to either side, so the clouds stop touching once d/s is above 6. In the last step the bracket `d` is divided into steps of length `s`: there are exactly 8, the first three reach from symbol a to the edge of its cloud, the last three from the edge of the other cloud to symbol b, and two stay free between them. In signal detection theory the same ratio is called d′ (d-prime, the sensitivity index), and it is a close relative of the signal-to-noise ratio. Your alphabet size measures nothing else, which is why it explains why the teams' answers to the opening question differ.

From this follows a sharper version of a phrase that is easily used loosely. **Safely distinguishable** does not mean that the means are different. It means that the clouds do not touch, not even at their outermost values. A single reading that slips across the boundary is a wrong symbol, and one wrong symbol in fifty attempts costs you an alphabet size.

**How many symbols fit? (Frames 38 to 40).** The left column shows the range, from 40 at the bottom to 840 at the top, and below it the subtraction that gives its width, 840 − 40 = 800. It stands from the start together with the column of wide bands, and the right one builds band by band. As soon as you think in bands instead of points, "how many colours can we use?" becomes a calculation. Measure the usable range of your link, from the darkest to the brightest value you can produce reliably, and the width of one cloud; the range divided by the width of one band is the number of symbols.

The example: the range runs from 40 to 840, so 800 units. If a colour scatters by plus or minus 100 around its mean, its band is 200 wide, not 100, because the cloud reaches in both directions, and four bands fill the range. If it scatters by only plus or minus 25, its band is 50 wide, and sixteen fit. Sixteen instead of four, because the clouds are four times narrower, and that answers the question from the beginning.

**Calibrate, then classify (Frames 41 to 44).** The clouds also tell the receiver which colour arrived. Calibrating means measuring known colours repeatedly under the conditions of your own setup, which gives clouds of reference values in a coordinate system. A new reading is then compared with these clouds and assigned to the closest one. In the example the red readings lie where the red share is high and the blue share low, the blue readings the other way round, and the green share is roughly constant and left out. On the slide the new reading `r=170, b=80` appears first, then its distances to both clouds are drawn, and the shorter one, to the red references, turns yellow while the reading takes on red. Nothing is calculated back to what the LED must have sent; the decision rests on your own measurements, and that is how a colour is recognised from numbers that never match it.

**Try it: the distinguishability lab (Frame 45).** The slide links the demonstrator [[distinguishability-lab]], a noisy channel drawn like an oscilloscope. Give the alphabet more symbols until the first errors appear, then lengthen the window until they are gone and see what is left of the symbol rate and the throughput. A disturbance shows what stray light does to a link, and which alphabet survives it.

**The toolbox, with price tags (Frame 46).** There are tools against noise, and hardly any of them are free. Above the two columns the two clouds return in small, as a reminder of d and s. On the left you enlarge the distance, on the right you shrink the spread, and almost every price tag says one of two things: fewer bits per symbol, or fewer symbols per second. Averaging in the program is the same bargain as a longer integration time in the sensor. Three tools are the exception, and they are the reason self-built optics are allowed in the LiFi project: aim the light better, block ambient light, fix distance and angle, meaning LED and sensor sit in one holder so that no reading changes because someone bumps the table or pulls a cable. They improve the ratio without you giving anything up and cost only an afternoon with cardboard, passive and ten euros at most. Whether a tool works on your own link, however, only a measurement series shows.

## Part 3: build evidence

**Three ways to answer the same question (Frames 48 to 50).** A plausible explanation says why something might hold. An AI prediction can even attach concrete numbers or an instruction to the claim. Both remain a claim, and only a measurement series on your own setup shows whether, and how strongly, the effect actually occurs. That is why, once the evidence appears, the first two boxes turn yellow with a warning sign. The example is the integration time: "longer measurements collect more light" sounds reasonable, and "double the integration time, and recognition improves" is a clear prediction, but whether the recognition rate on this setup really rises from 41 to 47 correct out of 50 is decided only by the trial.

**A claim becomes a test (Frames 51 to 55).** An experiment begins with a question. From it follows a prediction, which you write down before measuring. Then you measure under controlled conditions, in the example 50 symbols each with a short and with a long window, and finally you compare the result with the prediction: the hypothesis is supported or refuted. The order is decisive, because if you measure first and only afterwards decide which result counts as a success, you can read almost any outcome as a fit.

**Two teams, two changes (Frames 56 and 57).** Team A changes the distance and the integration time at the same time, Team B only the distance. Both see their recognition rate improve, but only Team B has learned what caused the improvement, while for Team A two possible causes remain mixed. So in a measurement series exactly one quantity is changed on purpose, and all other conditions stay as constant as possible and are written down.

**What makes a measurement series (Frames 58 to 60).** Before measuring, you write down the conditions and the expected effect. While measuring, you change exactly one quantity, take a control reading and repeat often enough. After measuring, you keep all the values, compare the result with the expectation and document the outcome. These three phases are also the checklist for the record: conditions make the series repeatable, the expectation makes it honest, one changed quantity makes it interpretable, repetitions make it reliable, and keeping every value stops only the pleasant results from surviving. A repeatable result is worth more than an impressive one.

**A coin flip: luck or skill? (Frames 61 to 63).** With two possible colours, every guess is a coin flip and gets every second decision right. Five hits in a row happen by chance in about one attempt in 32, so in a room with many teams a lucky streak like that quickly happens somewhere. Fifty hits in a row have a probability of roughly one in 1,125,899,906,842,624 when guessing, which is practically impossible, so reliable evidence needs long series.

**When the assistant and the data disagree (Frames 64 to 66).** The assistant suggests increasing the gain, because that is supposed to improve recognition, but the measurement series shows a drop from 94 to 82 per cent, because the scatter grows along with the signal. Before treating the suggestion as refuted, we check our own series: was only one quantity changed, were there enough repetitions, was there a control reading? If so, the series is the authoritative truth for your own setup, and the contradiction goes into the record with the suggestion, the measurement and the result, which turns the mistake into something learned. No language model has access to a measurement on your link, so none can settle a question about it.

**The measuring staff in the fog (Frame 67).** Everything in this deck is a property of **your** link: your room, your distance, your ambient light. Your task is not to defeat the noise, which cannot be done, but to measure it and then decide which alphabet and which speed your link honestly delivers. If you did not measure it, you do not know it: the fog stays, and what you hold in your hand is the yardstick.
