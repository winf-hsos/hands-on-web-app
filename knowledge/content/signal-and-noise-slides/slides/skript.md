# Lecture notes: signal and noise

These notes accompany the stagekit deck "signal and noise" and explain its contents for reading afterwards, in the order of the slides. Build-up steps and the segments of an animation count as frames of their own in the player and in the export (61 frames in all), so the references point to frames. The deck was put together on 2026-11-08 from the decks "why 16 and not 4?" and "measuring and experimenting".

The core message: how many symbols a link carries is not decided by its code but by the ratio of the distance between the symbols to the scatter of the readings. Every measure that improves this ratio has a price, and whether it works on your own setup is shown only by a clean measurement series.

The running example is a colour sensor that reads the light of an LED, as in a link that transmits data with light. Any other sensor behaves the same in principle.

## Part 1: what the numbers say

**The lighthouse in the fog (Frame 4).** The lamp burns exactly as brightly as on a clear night, and yet the fog swallows the beam after a few metres. The signal has not become weaker; the disturbance has become stronger. Every LiFi link is in the same position: all senders are equally bright and all sensors equally good, and still the teams get very different distances.

**Same hardware, different alphabets (Frame 5).** An example of five teams with the same LED, the same sensor and the same task: the size of the alphabet each reached, its hit rate, and what it did about noise. The last column is the most important one, because it already contains the answer before anyone says it. If you already measure your own link, add your team as a sixth row in your head. Then the question: with the same hardware and the same task, the largest and the smallest alphabet still differ by a factor of four, so where does the difference come from? To answer it, we first look at what arrives at the sensor at all.

**Four numbers instead of a colour (Frames 6 to 8).** The colour sensor delivers four values. Three channels measure through red, green and blue filters, and the fourth, called clear, measures all the incoming light without a filter, which makes it especially sensitive to bright and dark. The four numbers `r`, `g`, `b` and `c` are the program's actual input, and only processing turns them into the output `"red"`, `"blue"` or another symbol name. A sensor does not deliver the meaning of what it measures.

**Sent is not measured (Frames 9 and 10).** If you set the LED to `r=255, g=0, b=0`, it is easy to expect the same numbers at the sensor. On the slide the light travels across, and the four values count up at the sensor to something like `r=203, g=41, b=57, c=310`. That is not a defect but the normal case: the colour filters overlap, light gets lost with distance and angle, and room light reaches the sensor as well. A formula that calculates today's readings back to the LED values would already be wrong at the next table or on the next day, so we work with the readings of our own setup; part 2 shows how.

**Measure the dark first (Frames 11 to 14).** Even with its own LED switched off, the sensor does not show zero, because room light is always there. That is why every measurement series begins with a control reading with the LED switched off, the baseline against which all later values are read. A reading is made up of ambient light, the light of your own LED and noise, and the control reading estimates the first of them. It also tells you when the surroundings change during a series, for instance through afternoon sun or the LED of a neighbouring team.

**Ten readings of one colour (Frames 15 to 17).** The LED shows the same red ten times and nothing changes, yet the sensor reports ten different numbers. They arrive one after the other, and the newest is yellow while it is the current one. That surprises most people, because a computer is the one machine that reliably gives the same output for the same input. But a sensor never gets the same input twice: the light flickers, the electronics hiss, someone walks past the window. This variation is called **noise**, and every measuring instrument has it; ten identical values would be more suspicious than ten different ones.

In the last step the same ten values land on a number line, and you see what is left of them: a spread from 207 to 220. From this follows something that carries the whole deck: **a single reading says almost nothing.** The value 220 could be this colour, or a neighbouring colour in a bad moment, and only the ten together show where the colour really sits and how far it wanders.

**Centre and spread belong together (Frames 18 to 20).** The mean describes roughly where a series lies, but not how reliably a single value lies near it, and two series can have the same mean and very different spreads. That is why the individual values, or at least a measure of spread, belong in the record, and these two numbers, centre and spread, are what part 2 calculates with.

**Not the code but the scatter (Frame 21).** The punchline comes early, and the evidence follows. That the quality of the program decides almost nothing here surprises most people, and that is exactly the point. Noise is not a defect and not a bug but a property of every measurement, and whoever makes it smaller fits in more symbols.

## Part 2: what decides your alphabet

**The same signal, averaged over longer windows (Frames 23 to 26).** There is a way to make the numbers calmer: measure for longer and average what arrives. At the top is the raw signal as it reaches the sensor, far too restless for a decision in a single instant. Below it the same signal is averaged, first over 4, then over 12, then over 32 readings. In each strip a yellow line runs through the same stretch of time, and a mean appears as soon as its window is full, so the bars settle closer and closer to the true level.

The place to look is the right-hand edge, where the bars are counted: the same stretch of time holds 288 raw readings, then 72, 24 and 9 means. **Calmness is not free, you pay for it in readings per second**, and readings per second are exactly what your speed is made of. The spread also falls only with the square root of the window, so four times the window merely halves it. The sensor makes the same bargain with its integration time, the reading window over which it collects light. Gain is the second knob: it scales signal and noise alike and can drive the sensor into saturation.

**Two symbols, two clouds (Frames 27 and 28).** Now two symbols on the same scale, each not a single value but a cloud of values whose width is the noise from before. At first the clouds are wide, and the parts that reach across the boundary, drawn red, are exactly the readings the receiver assigns to the wrong symbol. Then the clouds narrow, as they do with a longer window or shielded light, and at the same distance `d` every reading lands on its own side. Neither the distance nor the width decides on its own.

**The ratio is what counts (Frame 29).** The quantity this deck is about is a quotient: the distance `d` between two symbols, divided by the spread `s` of the readings. Your alphabet size measures nothing else, which is why it explains the table from the beginning.

From this follows a sharper version of a phrase that is easily used loosely. **Safely distinguishable** does not mean that the means are different. It means that the clouds do not touch, not even at their outermost values. A single reading that slips across the boundary is a wrong symbol, and one wrong symbol in fifty attempts costs you an alphabet size.

**How many symbols fit? (Frames 30 to 32).** As soon as you think in bands instead of points, "how many colours can we use?" becomes a calculation. Measure the usable range of your link, from the darkest to the brightest value you can produce reliably, and the width of one cloud; the range divided by the width of one band is the number of symbols.

The example: the range runs from 40 to 840, so 800 units. If a colour scatters by plus or minus 100 around its mean, its band is 200 wide, not 100, because the cloud reaches in both directions, and four bands fill the range. If it scatters by only plus or minus 25, its band is 50 wide, and sixteen fit. Sixteen instead of four, because the clouds are four times narrower, and that answers the table from the beginning.

**Calibrate, then classify (Frames 33 to 36).** The clouds also tell the receiver which colour arrived. Calibrating means measuring known colours repeatedly under the conditions of your own setup, which gives clouds of reference values in a coordinate system. A new reading is then compared with these clouds and assigned to the closest one. In the example the red readings lie where the red share is high and the blue share low, the blue readings the other way round, and the green share is roughly constant and left out. On the slide the new reading `r=170, b=80` appears first, then its distances to both clouds are drawn, and the shorter one, to the red references, turns yellow while the reading takes on red. Nothing is calculated back to what the LED must have sent; the decision rests on your own measurements, and that is how a colour is recognised from numbers that never match it.

**Eight symbols, two window lengths (Frames 37 to 39).** Everything at once, for an alphabet of eight symbols. The gray dashes are the levels sent, the dots the readings, which arrive one after the other. With the short window six of thirty readings fall into another symbol's band and turn red, but forty symbols get through per second. With a window four times as long the readings scatter half as far, none lands in the wrong band, and only ten symbols get through per second. More symbols squeeze the bands together and faster symbols make the clouds wider, so both spend the same safety margin. The readings are simulated, but the structure is real. The Distinguishability Lab of the LiFi project lets you turn the same knobs yourself (https://demos.lifi-project.de/distinguishability-lab/); your own numbers come only from your own link.

**The toolbox, with price tags (Frame 40).** There are tools against noise, and hardly any of them are free. On the left you enlarge the distance, on the right you shrink the spread, and almost every price tag says one of two things: fewer bits per symbol, or fewer symbols per second. Averaging in the program is the same bargain as a longer integration time in the sensor. Three tools are the exception, and they are the reason self-built optics are allowed in the LiFi project: aim the light better, block ambient light, fix the geometry. They improve the ratio without you giving anything up and cost only an afternoon with cardboard, passive and ten euros at most. Whether a tool works on your own link, however, only a measurement series shows.

## Part 3: build evidence

**Three ways to answer the same question (Frames 42 to 44).** A plausible explanation says why something might hold. An AI prediction can even attach concrete numbers or an instruction to the claim. Both remain a claim, and only a measurement series on your own setup shows whether, and how strongly, the effect actually occurs. That is why, once the evidence appears, the first two boxes turn yellow with a warning sign. The example is the integration time: "longer measurements collect more light" sounds reasonable, and "double the integration time, and recognition improves" is a clear prediction, but whether the recognition rate on this setup really rises from 41 to 47 correct out of 50 is decided only by the trial.

**A claim becomes a test (Frames 45 to 48).** An experiment begins with a question. From it follows a prediction, which you write down before measuring. Then you measure under controlled conditions, in the example 50 symbols each with a short and with a long window, and finally you compare the result with the prediction: the hypothesis is supported or refuted. The order is decisive, because if you measure first and only afterwards decide which result counts as a success, you can read almost any outcome as a fit.

**Two teams, two changes (Frames 49 and 50).** Team A changes the distance and the integration time at the same time, Team B only the distance. Both see their recognition rate improve, but only Team B has learned what caused the improvement, while for Team A two possible causes remain mixed. So in a measurement series exactly one quantity is changed on purpose, and all other conditions stay as constant as possible and are written down.

**What makes a measurement series (Frames 51 to 53).** Before measuring, you write down the conditions and the expected effect. While measuring, you change exactly one quantity, take a control reading and repeat often enough. After measuring, you keep all the values, compare the result with the expectation and document the outcome. These three phases are also the checklist for the record: conditions make the series repeatable, the expectation makes it honest, one changed quantity makes it interpretable, repetitions make it reliable, and keeping every value stops only the pleasant results from surviving. A repeatable result is worth more than an impressive one.

**Luck or skill? (Frames 54 to 56).** With two possible colours, pure guessing gets every second decision right. Five hits in a row happen by chance in about one attempt in 32, so in a room with many teams a lucky streak like that quickly happens somewhere. Fifty hits in a row have a probability of roughly one in 1,125,899,906,842,624 when guessing, which is practically impossible, so reliable evidence needs long series.

**When the assistant and the data disagree (Frames 57 to 60).** The assistant suggests increasing the gain, because that is supposed to improve recognition, but the measurement series shows a drop from 94 to 82 per cent, because the scatter grows along with the signal. Before treating the suggestion as refuted, we check our own series: was only one quantity changed, were there enough repetitions, was there a control reading? If so, the series is the authoritative truth for your own setup, and the contradiction goes into the log of mistakes with the suggestion, the measurement and the result. No language model has access to a measurement on your link, so none can settle a question about it.

**The measuring staff in the fog (Frame 61).** Everything in this deck is a property of **your** link: your room, your distance, your ambient light. Your task is not to defeat the noise, which cannot be done, but to measure it and then decide which alphabet and which speed your link honestly delivers. If you did not measure it, you do not know it: the fog stays, and what you hold in your hand is the yardstick.
