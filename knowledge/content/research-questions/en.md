A survey with hundreds of columns answers nothing by itself. Whoever looks
into it without a question finds patterns that are often accidental and in
the end cannot say what the work has found out. The research question is
therefore the part of an empirical paper on which everything else depends:
the introduction leads up to it, the method describes how it is answered,
and the discussion begins with the answer. This lesson draws on two books
you can read further: the chapter "Forschungsthema" by Nicola Döring and the
chapter "Research Questions" by Nick Huntington-Klein.

## Topic, problem, question

A **topic** only names a subject, such as "plant-based milk". The **research
problem** states which insight one wants to gain about which aspect, for
instance how the price affects the choice between oat drink and cow's milk.
The **research question** turns this into something data can answer: "How
does purchase certainty for Alpro oat drink change when its price rises from
1.79 to 2.09 euros?" Döring describes this path as narrowing down step by
step: to a target group, a period and particular characteristics, with an
eye on what has already been researched and on the method one works with
(Döring 2023, pp. 148–149).

The most common mistake at the start is to stop at the topic. "We study
sustainability in buying milk" sounds like a plan but is not yet a question,
because nobody can say when it would be answered.

## Two conditions

Huntington-Klein sets two conditions for a research question
(*The Effect*, section 2.1):

1. **It can be answered with evidence.** "Which milk ad is the best?" fails
   because "the best" does not say how one would measure it. "Which ad makes
   more people want to buy the product?" can be checked with data.
2. **The answer says something about how the world works.** "How many
   respondents live in Lower Saxony?" can be answered, but the answer
   explains nothing. "Do environmentally conscious people buy a larger share
   of organic food?" says something about attitudes and behaviour.

A good touchstone: would an unexpected result change your mind? If you
could explain away every possible result in advance, you learn nothing from
the question.

## Question or hypothesis

A **hypothesis** is a claim with a direction, such as "respondents over 60
buy a larger share of organic food than respondents under 30". It is not a
gut feeling but has to be derived from a theory or from earlier findings
that one cites (Döring 2023, pp. 149–150). Where this prior knowledge is
missing, one asks an open **research question** that leaves the direction
open, for the same example "does the share of organic food people buy differ
by age?". Whoever analyses an existing survey from a new
angle usually works with questions; you only state a hypothesis if you can
back it with literature.

## Three kinds of questions

What you may claim at the end depends on the kind of question.

| Kind | asks for | Example from the survey |
|---|---|---|
| descriptive | how many, how often, how distributed | How are respondents distributed across diets? |
| relational | whether groups differ or two characteristics occur together | Does the share of organic food bought differ between age groups? |
| causal | what happens if one thing is changed | Does a higher price lower purchase certainty for Alpro oat drink? |

Describing and comparing works with any survey. You may only claim a cause
if the design separates the cause from everything else, most safely through
an experiment in which chance decided who saw what. The finer division into
six types after Leek and Peng is shown in the lesson
[[exploratory-data-analysis-basics]].

## How to recognise a good question

Huntington-Klein gives a checklist of five points (*The Effect*,
section 2.4) that you hold against your own question before you compute
anything:

1. **Potential results:** would every plausible answer teach something,
   including the one you do not expect?
2. **Feasibility:** do the data contain what you need?
3. **Scale:** can the question be answered in the time you have?
4. **Design:** can these data separate what you want to separate?
5. **Simplicity:** is it one question or three?

From the perspective of choosing a topic, Döring adds personal interest,
prior knowledge, scientific and practical relevance and empirical
researchability, whose limits include ethical concerns and the effort for
respondents and researchers (Döring 2023, pp. 152–157).

Points three and five fail most often. "What influences the purchase of
organic food?" sounds more important than "Do respondents who prefer organic
products shop at discounters less often than others?", but the first
question is a whole research programme and the second one you can answer in
four weeks.

## Questions without an answer

Some questions sound plausible but cannot be answered with data, or not
with these data. Four traps come up again and again:

- **Normative:** "Should oat drink cost as much as cow's milk?" Data
  describe, they do not judge. What can be answered is how many respondents
  think so.
- **Vague:** "What do Germans think about food?" The subject and what is to
  be measured are missing; besides, the survey is a quota-based online panel
  of people who do their own shopping, not "the Germans".
- **Causal without design:** "Does a higher income make people buy
  organic?" In a one-off survey, income goes together with education, place
  of residence and values, and the data cannot separate these influences.
  What can be answered is whether income and the organic share are related.
- **Not in the data:** "Do people buy more organic today than in 2020?" The
  survey is a single wave, and without a second point in time there is no
  comparison over time.

A trap rarely means that the idea is bad. Usually the question can be
rescued: "makes" becomes "is related to", "should" becomes "how many think",
and the report says honestly why one cannot claim more.

## From idea to question

A vague idea becomes a question when you can give six pieces of
information:

| Information | for the example "prices and oat drink" |
|---|---|
| who | people who bought and drank milk or milk alternatives in the last twelve months |
| where | an online panel across Germany |
| when | November 2025 |
| which variation | the price of Alpro oat drink: 1.69, 1.79, 1.89 or 2.09 euros |
| which outcome | purchase certainty on a scale from −4 to +4 |
| which comparison | each price level against the regular price of 1.79 euros |

This becomes: "How does purchase certainty for Alpro oat drink change among
people who buy and drink milk when its price is 10 cents below or 10 or 30 cents above the
regular price of 1.79 euros?" In the survey, 601 people saw a price and 596
of them rated Alpro. Whoever gets stuck on one of the six pieces
knows where their own question is still blurred.

This example is also the one place in the survey where a causal question can
be answered: chance determined which price level a person saw, and the
prices of the competing products stayed the same. That is why the groups
differ only in the price, and a difference in purchase certainty can be
traced back to the price.

## The reality test

In many textbooks the question comes first and the data collection after.
When analysing existing data it is the other way round: the data have been
collected, and no question can create a column afterwards. So the question
adapts to the data and not the other way round. That is not a makeshift but
the normal case in applied market research, and Huntington-Klein explicitly
names an existing data set as a legitimate occasion for a question, as long
as one can say why it matters (*The Effect*, section 2.3).

The test goes like this: break your question into its parts and look up the
column for each part in the codebook, the documentation of the data that
lists every column with its question, who was asked and what each code
means. Check who was asked, because many
questions of the survey went only to part of the respondents. At the end
there is one of three results: the question can be answered, it has to be
adapted, or it is dropped. The mistake is not adapting the question but
talking the data into fitting, for instance when a column that measures
something else has to serve as a substitute.

your question as it is now, and give it to the agent with this task:

```text
Here is our research question: <your question>
Break it into who, where, when, which variation, which outcome and which
comparison. Find the matching column in the codebook for each part and tell
us who was asked and how many answered. Tell us which part is missing or
blurred, and suggest two sharpened versions that the data can answer. Do not
compute anything yet.
```

Check the answer before you take it over: look up every column it names in
the codebook yourself and read the question text. At the end, write down:

1. your sharpened question in one sentence,
2. the columns that answer it, with filter and number of cases,
3. what you changed compared with the first version and why.

## Takeaways

- A topic names a subject, a research question can be answered with data,
  and a hypothesis is a justified claim with a direction.
- A research question has to be answerable with evidence and say something
  about how the world works.
- What you may claim depends on the kind of question: describing and
  comparing always works, a cause only with a design that separates it.
- Normative, vague, overreaching causal questions and questions not covered
  by the data can usually be rebuilt into answerable ones.
- Who, where, when, which variation, which outcome, which comparison: six
  pieces of information turn an idea into a question.
- With existing data, the question adapts to the data, and the codebook is
  the touchstone.
