Explorers in the past did not know what they would find; they walked, drew what they saw and marked what they did not understand yet. Exploring data works the same way: you start without knowing what is in the table, you draw a first picture, and the picture tells you where to look next. What you bring home is a better map and a list of good questions, not yet the answers.
First the big picture: where exploring sits in a data analysis.

the workflow of a data analysis

after Wickham, Çetinkaya-Rundel & Grolemund: R for Data Science, 2nd ed.
exploring data is a loop, not a step.
Every analysis loads data and brings it into a tidy shape; that is preparation. Understanding happens in the loop: you transform the data, look at it in a picture, perhaps fit a simple model, and every picture sends you back with a new idea. Only at the end do you communicate, and programming holds it all together so that every step can be repeated.
exploratory data analysis is a state of mind, not a fixed procedure.
R for Data Science: generate questions, search for answers by transforming and visualising, and use what you learn to refine the questions.
There is no checklist that makes an exploration complete. What R for Data Science describes is a cycle of three moves: generate questions about your data, search for answers by transforming and visualising, and use what you learn to refine the questions or ask new ones. The quality of an exploration depends on the questions you bring into it.

two kinds of questions

almost every exploratory question asks how values vary, or how two variables vary together.
These two questions from R for Data Science are a good starting point when you face a new table and do not know where to begin. Variation is the spread of values within one column: which values occur, which are common, which are rare. Covariation is the tendency of two columns to vary together. The third part asks both of the milk survey.
Before looking at data, it helps to know which type of question you are asking, because the type decides what you may claim at the end.

the wrong question, answered correctly

many analyses that are criticised later did not compute anything wrong, they answered a different question than the one people read into them.
Leek & Peng (2015): What is the question? Science 347, 1314–1315
This is the observation behind a two-page paper by Jeffery Leek and Roger Peng. A pattern found while exploring is reported as if it held for everyone, or a good prediction is read as an explanation. To avoid it, they suggest deciding which type of question you are asking before you start.

six yes-or-no questions

after Leek & Peng (2015), Science 347, 1314–1315
You start at the top and answer until you reach a type. If you did not summarise the data, for example because you only show single rows, it is not yet a data analysis. A summary reported without interpretation is descriptive. If you do not check whether your discoveries would hold in a new sample, the analysis is exploratory, and that is the type of this deck. If you do check it, the next question is whether you want to know how changing one quantity changes another. If not, you either predict values for individuals, which is predictive and the field of machine learning, or you test whether a pattern in the sample is systematic rather than chance, which is inferential. If you do, an effect on average is causal, found best with experiments and the methods of causal inference, and an effect that occurs in exactly this way in every single case is mechanistic. The two questions that matter most are whether a pattern is meant to be more than chance and whether you are asking for a cause. The tree is strict on purpose: it names what you are entitled to claim at the end.

six types, asked of one survey

typewhat you doa question for the milk survey
descriptivecount and summarisehow many drink milk every day?
exploratorylook for patternsare the young more open to oat milk?
inferentialtest a pattern against chanceis this a real pattern or due to chance?
predictivepredict for individualscan age and diet predict oat milk?
causalchange one thing, watch anotherdoes a higher price change the choice?
mechanisticunderstand each single casehow much energy heats a litre of milk?
The examples are those of the lesson on analytic questions. 1,452 of the 2,387 people asked drink milk every day, which answers the descriptive question. The inferential question asks whether the age pattern is real or due to chance, and a significance test on these data answers it; whether it holds for all of Germany is a different question, about how well the sample represents the country. The causal question can actually be answered with this survey, because the price of alpro oat drink was an experiment: chance decided whether a respondent saw it ten cents cheaper, at its normal price, or ten or thirty cents dearer, and everyone said how surely they would buy it. The mechanistic one cannot be answered by any survey; it belongs to physics.
which type of question is this: “what share of the respondents buys (almost) only organic food?”
  1. descriptive
  2. exploratory
  3. inferential
  4. causal
Descriptive: the question counts and summarises without interpreting. The answer is 111 of 2,793 people, about 4 percent. It would turn inferential if we asked whether a difference in this share, say between age groups, is a real pattern or due to chance.

the step people skip

the data stay the same, only the claim grows, and with it what you would have to show.
The most common mistake is not a wrong calculation but a silent promotion. The first sentence is exploratory and honest: in our survey, younger people appear more open to oat milk. The second claims that the difference is systematic and not chance; that is inferential and needs a significance test on these data, which nobody ran. The third says that being young causes it; that is causal and would need an experiment. Nothing in the data changed between the three sentences; only the claim did. Whether any of it holds for all of Germany is yet another question, about how well the sample represents the country.

search long enough, and you will find something

so write down your questions and what theory leads you to expect before you collect data, not after searching it.
With 606 columns there are 183,315 pairs of variables. To see what chance alone produces, shuffle every column on its own, so that no column has anything to do with any other, and test every pair that can be tested: about 7,000 pairs still pass a test at the 5 percent level, with correlations of up to about 0.5. That is no reason not to explore, but it is the reason to write down your questions, and the expectations you derive from theory, before you collect the data. A hypothesis stated in advance is tested once, while a pattern found by searching had thousands of chances to appear, which is why an exploratory finding is a question for the next study and not its result.
exploring finds questions worth asking, it does not answer them.
This is the sentence of the whole deck. Everything you find while exploring deserves a name that says what it is: a pattern in these data, worth checking.
Now a first exploration of the survey on milk and chocolate: 2,811 people who shop for their household, 606 columns. The columns used here carry readable names instead of the codes of the questionnaire; loading the data, renaming and selecting the columns and saving the result is an exercise of its own.

half of the table is empty

in a survey, missing usually means “not asked”, and dropping those rows changes who you are talking about.
Here is the whole table, one pixel per cell, answered cells light, empty ones dark. Almost exactly half of all cells are empty, and almost none of them because someone refused to answer. Many questions went only to a part of the respondents: the chocolate questions to 275 people, the milk questions to 234, and those two groups sit at the top of the picture. Remove every row with a missing value, and nobody is left, because every single row has gaps; remove them for one question, and you are talking about a different group than you think.

variation: how old are the respondents?

ggplot(survey, aes(x = age)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 5)
ages from 18 to 80 with the middle at 50: a quota sample, not a random one.
The histogram is the first tool for variation: what values occur, which are common, which are rare. All 2,811 people gave their age, from 18 to 80, with a median of 50. The shape is no accident; the panel provider filled quotas for age, gender and federal state. That matters whenever a result is meant to hold for Germany as a whole.

unusual values: when is milk too expensive?

ggplot(survey, aes(x = price_too_expensive)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 10) ggplot(survey, aes(x = price_too_expensive)) + geom_histogram(binwidth = 0.1) + coord_cartesian(xlim = c(0, 5))
one strange value can hide a whole distribution, so look at the extremes before you look at the average.
612 people said from which price on a litre of milk is too expensive for them. The first picture is useless: a single bar, because a few answers up to 999 stretch the axis to a thousand euros. Zoom in with coord_cartesian(), and the real distribution appears, with its middle at 1.99 euros. Then look at the extreme values themselves: 16 answers lie above 20 euros. A 160 is almost certainly 1.60 typed without the point, a 344 perhaps 3.44, and 999 is a common code for "don't know".

mean or median?

the median barely notices a typing error, while the mean is dragged along by it.
With all 612 answers the mean is 8.11 euros, a price at which nobody in the survey would really stop buying milk; it is pulled up by the 16 extreme answers. Set only those answers to NA, which keeps everything else these people said, and the mean drops to 2.17 euros. The median stays at 1.99 euros in both cases, because it only depends on the middle of the sorted values. When a distribution has extreme values, report the median, and say which values you set to NA and why, because the threshold is a judgement.

covariation: organic food and diet

ggplot(survey, aes(x = diet, y = organic_share)) + geom_boxplot()
the groups differ, and the smallest group differs most, which is exactly where chance plays the biggest part.
A categorical and a numerical variable: the boxplot is the standard picture. The median share of organic food rises from 2 for omnivores to 3 for flexitarians, vegetarians and pescetarians and 4 for vegans. Read the group sizes before the boxes: 1,816 omnivores, but only 36 vegans. A striking box built on a handful of people is a reason for a closer look, not for a headline.

covariation: oat milk and age

survey |> filter(!is.na(no_oat_milk)) |> mutate(age_group = cut(age, c(17, 29, 44, 59, 80))) |> ggplot(aes(x = age_group, fill = no_oat_milk == 0)) + geom_bar(position = "fill")
a pattern in 234 people is a question for the next study, not yet an answer.
This is the exploratory question from the table of types. In yellow the share of people who would buy oat milk at all: 68 percent of the youngest group, 38 percent of those over 60. The picture suggests an answer, but it rests on 234 people from a quota sample, and the youngest group holds only 34 of them. Whether the difference is a real pattern or due to chance is the inferential question, and a significance test on these data answers it; whether it holds for everyone in Germany depends on how well the quota sample represents the country.
respondents saw alpro oat drink at a price chosen by chance and said how surely they would buy it. which type of question can this answer?
  1. descriptive
  2. exploratory
  3. predictive
  4. causal
Causal: on a shelf next to Weihenstephan at 1.79 euros and Gut & Günstig at 1.09, alpro cost 1.69, 1.79, 1.89 or 2.09 euros, and chance decided which of the four a respondent saw. For each of the three milks they said how surely they would buy it, from minus 4 for certainly not to plus 4 for certainly. Because chance decided who saw which price, the groups differ only in the price, and a difference in how surely they would buy alpro can be put down to it. That is the idea of an experiment, and it is the strongest way to answer a causal question.

from exploring to the next question

the result of an exploration is a list of better questions.
Three findings from this exploration and the questions they raise. That younger respondents seem more open to oat milk raises the inferential question whether this is a real pattern or due to chance. That vegans and vegetarians buy more organic food raises a causal one: is it the diet, or something behind both, such as income? And that people name very different prices from which a litre of milk is too expensive raises a predictive question: can age and diet predict a person's price limit?
