Four tools that all produce numbers. A length you may double: a board of two metres is twice as long as one of one metre. A temperature in degrees Celsius you may not double: ten degrees are not twice as warm as five. The kitchen scale behaves like the ruler, and the strokes on the tally sheet only count how often something happened. The numbers look alike, but they allow different questions, and this deck is about telling them apart.
First the words for what we built when we wrote down our games: rows, columns and cells.

one row per game, one column per fact

an observation is one game, and a variable is one fact that can differ from game to game.
after Döring (2023), p. 577, and R for Data Science, chapter "data tidying"
This is the table we built in the room, with four games. First key: one row is one game, an observation, which Döring calls a case. Second key: one column is one fact about every game, a variable; here the door with the car. Third key: where a row meets a column there is exactly one value, here the decision in game 4. Cases in the rows, variables in the columns, one value per cell: that is the order R for Data Science calls tidy and Döring calls a data matrix, and we had already built it before we knew its name.

a variable varies

a fact that is the same in every game is a constant, and a constant needs no column.
Döring defines a variable as a feature that can take different values. The decision is a variable: it is stay in one game and switch in the next. The number of doors is three in every game, so it is a constant; it belongs in the description of the study, not in the table. One more pair of words in passing: some variables can be observed directly, such as the door that was opened, others only through indicators, such as how willing someone is to take a risk. The first kind is called manifest, the second latent, and questionnaires are mostly about latent ones.
Not every column of numbers is a column of quantities. The four scales of measurement say what a value may mean.

types of variables

after Döring (2023), chapter 8.4
Three branches first. Categorical variables sort observations into groups, numerical variables measure an amount, and qualitative variables hold free text that has to be read before it can be counted. On the key, the scales of measurement, which Döring calls Skalenniveaus: a categorical variable is nominal if its groups have no order and ordinal if they do; a numerical variable is on an interval scale if only differences are meaningful and on a ratio scale if it has a true zero. On the key, one example each: the decision in our game, the share of organic food in the milk survey from 1 to 5, a year, a price, and a free comment. Döring also calls interval and ratio together metric. Some books split numerical variables into continuous ones and counts instead; that matters for R and comes back in part 3.

what each scale allows

each scale keeps everything the one above it allows and adds one more question you may ask.
Row by row. Nominal values may only be compared for equal or not equal: two games with the same decision. Ordinal values add an order: 4 is more organic than 2, but we do not know by how much. Interval values add equal distances: from 1990 to 2000 is as long as from 2000 to 2010. Ratio values add a true zero, and only then is a statement like "twice as much" meaningful: a milk at 2 euros costs twice as much as one at 1 euro. Döring adds a useful rule: you may always step down a scale, for example turn prices into the groups cheap and expensive, but never up.

door 3 is not three times door 1

a number used as a name is still a name, and its mean means nothing.
The doors carry numbers, but the numbers are names: we could just as well call them left, middle and right. R does not know that and computes the mean of the prize doors without complaint, here 2. The sentence underneath, that the average car stands behind door 2, is nonsense. Döring warns of exactly this: statistics software computes meaningless means whenever categories are coded as numbers. A coding plan, which writes down what each number stands for, is the defence, and in R so is the type of the column, as part 3 shows.

ten degrees are not twice as warm as five

ratios need a true zero, which a price has and a temperature in Celsius does not.
On the left two thermometers at 5 and 10 degrees Celsius, and 10 looks like twice 5. On the right two prices, 1 and 2 euros. On the key, both change their unit, while the things themselves stay the same: the temperatures become 41 and 50 degrees Fahrenheit, and suddenly nothing is twice anything; the prices become 100 and 200 cents, and the second is still twice the first. Zero degrees Celsius is just where water freezes, a point someone picked, whereas zero cents is truly nothing. In practice the difference between interval and ratio rarely matters, because few methods need a ratio scale, which is why Döring often puts both together as metric.

is a rating scale ordinal or interval?

if in doubt, compute both, and say which one you report.
The milk survey asks for the share of organic food from 1, hardly any, to 5, almost all. Strictly, only the order is known, so the scale is ordinal and the median is the right summary. Many researchers treat such rating scales with five or more roughly equal steps as interval and report the mean, and Döring describes this as common practice. For the 30 people in our sample the median is 2 and the mean 2.37, and both tell the same story. If in doubt, compute both; they usually agree, and when they do not, that is worth reporting.
R knows nothing about scales of measurement, but it has data types, and with the right type a column says what it is.

every scale has its type in R

a factor is how R remembers that a column is categorical.
The same tree, now with the types R uses, written the way a tibble prints them. First key: nominal values are text, chr, logical values for yes and no, lgl, or a factor, fct; ordinal values are an ordered factor, ord. Second key: numerical values are doubles, dbl, or integers, int, which is the natural type for counts and whole numbers such as years. Third key: free text stays text. The colours are the colours of the types in the other decks, with the factor added in red. The important type here is the factor: it is the only one that tells R that a column is categorical.

telling R what a column is

library(dplyr) monty_hall <- monty_hall |> mutate(decision = factor(decision, levels = c("stay", "switch"))) survey <- survey |> mutate(organic_share = factor(organic_share, levels = 1:5, ordered = TRUE))
factor() marks a column as nominal, ordered = TRUE as ordinal, and numbers stay double or integer.
Two lines, one per scale. The decision becomes a factor with the levels stay and switch, which makes it nominal. The organic share becomes an ordered factor with the levels 1 to 5, which makes it ordinal. Numerical columns stay as they are. From then on tables and plots sort the levels in their order, a typo becomes NA instead of a new category, and mean() on a factor returns NA with a warning instead of a meaningless number.

what you may compute

the scale tells you which function makes sense, while R will run all of them anyway.
Row by row, each new row in white and what it inherits in gray. Nominal: count how often each value occurs and show it as a bar chart. Ordinal: in addition the median and quantiles, and bars in the order of the scale. Interval: in addition the mean and the standard deviation, and a histogram or a boxplot. Ratio: in addition ratios, such as a new price divided by an old one. R will compute every one of these functions on any column of numbers; choosing the right one is your job.
