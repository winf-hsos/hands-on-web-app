Hardly anyone reads a paper from start to finish. Whoever picks up a paper
reads title and abstract, then often jumps to the discussion and only after
that, if at all, to the method. That only works because almost all empirical
papers are built the same way: each part has one job and stands where the
reader expects it. This lesson shows what that job is and draws mainly on
the chapter "Ergebnispräsentation" by Nicola Döring.

![The textbook for this lesson](item:doering-research-methods)

## Four parts, four questions

The structure is named **IMRaD** after its initials:

| Part | answers the question |
|---|---|
| **I**ntroduction | Why does this matter, and what is the question? |
| **M**ethods | How did we find out? |
| **R**esults | What did we find? |
| **D**iscussion | What does it mean? |

Title and abstract come before, the references after. Döring lists the state
of research as a part of its own between introduction and method; many
international journals fold it into the introduction (Döring 2023, p. 770).
The structure became established between 1940 and 1980, in the large
medical journals from single articles to the rule, because editors demanded
it: readers can jump straight to the part they need (Sollaci and Pereira
2004).

The length follows the job as well. For a report of 20 pages, Döring gives
the guideline of one page introduction, four pages theory and state of
research, two pages method, six pages results, three pages discussion and
four pages references; twelve pages of theory and two pages of results she
calls unbalanced (Döring 2023, pp. 770–771).

## The hourglass

A paper has the shape of an hourglass. The **introduction** starts wide with
the world, narrows via what is known to the gap and ends in its last
paragraph with the question. **Methods** and **results** are narrow and stay
with this one question. The **discussion** begins with the answer and then
widens again: comparison with other studies, limits of one's own work,
conclusions. That is why whoever reads only the last paragraph of the
introduction and the first of the discussion knows what it is about.

## What goes where

**Introduction.** In go why the topic matters, what is already known, which
gap remains and, at the end, the question. Not in go an overview of the
whole field, results and a question that only appears later.

**Methods.** It is a recipe: could someone repeat the same analysis with
these details? In go the data source, who was asked and how respondents
were recruited, the questions you use, how you prepared the data, for
instance which values you excluded, and how you analysed. If you analyse an
existing survey, you state explicitly that it is a secondary analysis and
who collected the data; the APA reporting standards ask for this too. Not in
goes what a mean is: standard procedures are named, not explained.

**Results.** In goes what you found, in the order of your questions, with
numbers, tables and figures. Each finding appears exactly once, in the text,
in a table or in a figure, and each figure is understandable on its own with
its caption (Döring 2023, p. 772). Not in go explanations of why something
is the case and evaluative words like "remarkably".

**Discussion.** After the abstract it is the part experts read first
(Döring 2023, p. 770). It begins with the answer to the question from the
introduction, compares it with other studies, names the limits of one's own
work in a section of its own and draws conclusions. Not in go new results,
repeating all the numbers and "more research is needed" as the only
conclusion.

## The most common mistake: interpretation in the results

The mistake found most often in student papers is a sentence like this in
the results: "Vegetarians name a higher price threshold, probably because
animal welfare matters to them." The first half is a finding the data show:
in the milk survey, the median of the 28 vegetarians who named a plausible
price threshold is 2.45 euros, that of 362 respondents who eat everything
1.99 euros. The second half is an interpretation the data do not show, because
animal welfare was not asked about at this point.

The solution is to split the sentence. The finding stays in the results with
its numbers. The interpretation moves to the discussion, is marked there as
a conjecture and compared with other studies that support or contradict it,
and that is also where the remark belongs that 28 people are a small group.
That way every reader can tell what you measured and what you conclude from
it.

## The abstract

The abstract is the whole paper in one paragraph of about 150 to 250 words,
with roughly one sentence each on problem, question, method, main findings
and conclusion (Döring 2023, p. 776). It is written last, when you know what
the work has found. Many journals ask for a structured abstract with fixed
subheadings such as *Purpose*, *Design*, *Findings* and *Originality*; even
without subheadings this order helps you.

The title names the most important variables and the group studied. Döring
recommends the topic as the main title and the method as the subtitle
(Döring 2023, p. 771).

## Exercise: dissect a real paper

The following paper uses an online survey to study why people buy
plant-based milk and is structured by IMRaD throughout. It is open access
and short enough to search in half an hour.

![The paper for the exercise](item:dorina-plant-based-milk)

Do not read it from the start, but answer these questions in this order:

1. Where is the research question, and what is it? Look at the end of the
   introduction.
2. Where is the answer? Look at the beginning of the discussion.
3. Who was asked, how many, and how were respondents recruited?
4. Which limits do the authors name themselves?
5. Do you find a sentence in the results that interprets instead of
   reporting, or a number in the discussion that did not appear in the
   results?
6. Check the means and standard deviations at the beginning of the results:
   does every standard deviation fit the scale on which the variable was
   measured?

## Takeaways

- Introduction, methods, results and discussion answer four questions: why,
  how, what and what it means.
- The introduction ends with the question, the discussion begins with the
  answer.
- The method is a recipe; with existing data it includes the data source
  and the note that it is a secondary analysis.
- Each finding appears exactly once in the results, and every
  interpretation belongs in the discussion.
- The abstract is the whole paper in one paragraph and is written last.
