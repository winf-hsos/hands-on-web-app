Before you play the game on a computer, you play it together at the front of
the classroom: with three doors, a host who knows where the car is, and a
different person in every game who gets to guess. The rest of the room votes,
watches and keeps the record. After ten games or more you have a first small
data set and a feeling for what actually happens in a game. What the game
looked like on television is shown in
[[geh-aufs-ganze|Geh aufs Ganze: the game on television]].

## What you need

- three doors that can stand side by side and be opened one at a time, marked
  1, 2 and 3 in large print: three large envelopes, three upturned boxes or
  three sheets held to the board with magnets
- three cards that fit behind the doors: one with a car, two with a goat
- a die and a coin
- a screen behind which the cards are placed, such as the lectern or an
  open flip chart
- the board or a computer on the projector for the record; the table from
  [[what-should-we-measure]] works well for this

## Setup

At the front the three doors stand side by side on a table, their numbers
facing the room. The host stands behind the table: before every game he
places the cards and is the only one who knows where the car is. In front of
the table stands the person who guesses, someone different from the room in
every game. Another person keeps the record at the board, and everyone else is
the audience.

![The floor plan of the front of a classroom. At the top the board with the record, and in front of it the person keeping the record. Below that a table with three closed doors numbered 1, 2 and 3, and behind the table the host with die and coin. In front of the table stands the person who guesses, and below sits the audience in three rows.](figure:classroom-setup "Who stands where: the host knows the car, the person in front of the table guesses, and the record keeps every game.")

For the experiment to be worth anything, chance decides, not the host. The die
decides where the car goes: on 1 or 2 behind door 1, on 3 or 4 behind door 2,
on 5 or 6 behind door 3. If both doors he may open hide goats, he tosses the
coin: on heads he opens the one with the lower number. A host who likes to
put the car behind door 2, or an audience that watches the cards being
placed, spoils the games.

## Before: your expectation

Before the first game begins, the whole room votes by show of hands: do you
win more often if you stay with your door, if you switch, or does it make no
difference? The record keeps the three numbers.

## How a game goes

![A game in five rows, each with the three doors. First, hide: behind the screen the car is behind door 2, the goats behind 1 and 3. Second, choose: door 1 is marked. Third, open: door 3 stands open and shows a goat. Fourth, decide: an arrow from door 1 to door 2 asks whether to switch. Fifth, reveal: the person stayed, door 1 hides a goat, and the game is lost.](figure:one-game "A game in five steps, here one in which the person stays with door 1 and loses.")

1. **Hide.** Behind the screen the host rolls the die, puts the car behind
   the door it shows and the goats behind the other two. Then he sets up the
   closed doors.
2. **Choose.** The person at the front points to a door, which stays closed
   for now.
3. **Open.** The host opens one of the other two doors, always one with a
   goat. If he has two to choose from, the coin decides.
4. **Decide.** The host asks: "Do you stay, or do you switch?" The audience
   may first show by hand what it would do, but the decision is made by the
   person at the front alone.
5. **Reveal.** All doors open, and the record notes the game.

Then the next person comes to the front. Play ten games or more: the more
there are, the less the result depends on the luck of single games. Make sure
that staying and switching happen about equally often. If one of the two
decisions is clearly rarer, decide beforehand for the last games what the
person at the front will do.

## Record

Every game gets one row. The first three could look like this:

| game | car behind | first choice | opened | decision | won |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | 2 | 1 | stay | yes |
| 2 | 3 | 1 | 2 | switch | yes |
| 3 | 1 | 1 | 3 | switch | no |

Whether these six columns hold everything you will want to know later, and
whether one of them already follows from the others, you discuss in
[[what-should-we-measure]].

## Analysis

- How often did the people who stayed win, and how often those who switched,
  each as a proportion? Does that fit your vote before the first game?
- How often was the first choice already right? What proportion would you
  have expected?
- In which games did the host have to toss the coin, and in which did he have
  no choice at all? What did he know in those games that the person at the
  front did not?
- Would you say after your games which decision is the better one? How many
  games would you need to be sure?

On your own you then go on in the [[monty-hall-game|three-door game]], and in
phase 4 you let R play so many games that the last question answers itself.

> [!NOTE]
> Without a classroom it works for two at a table: three cups and a small
> object under one of them, such as an eraser. Whoever hosts rolls the die
> unseen, hides the object and afterwards lifts an empty cup.
