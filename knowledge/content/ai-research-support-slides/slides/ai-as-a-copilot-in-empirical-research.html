First the map: the steps of a study with data that someone else has already collected, as in a secondary analysis of a survey.

seven steps of a study

The first row plans the study: you find a question, read what is known about it and check which data and which columns of the codebook can answer it. The second row works with the data: you analyse, draw figures, write the paper and present it. In the last step the colours show the division of labour. In the blue steps an AI can do much of the work, such as searching, writing R code, drawing a chart or polishing a paragraph, and your job is to check it. In the white steps you decide, and an AI is at best a sparring partner: the question, the choice of data and what you stand up and say are yours. Alongside all of this there is housekeeping, such as references, file names and formatting, where an AI saves a lot of time.
Now five of the steps one by one. Each slide shows one request as you could type it, on the milk survey, and the checks that turn an answer into something you can use. The requests are written so that the AI helps without taking the decision away from you.

research question

The AI knows the codebook only if you give it to it, and then it is good at matching a question to columns. Ask it to look, not to rewrite: the last sentence of the request keeps the question yours. Then check that the columns it names really exist, and that the question still asks what you wanted to know and not what is easiest to answer.

literature

For the literature, ask for search terms and places to look rather than for finished references, and then search the library catalogue and a scholarly database yourself. A reference that a chat app writes down can look perfect and still not exist; part three shows how often that happened. Only what you have opened and read goes into your paper.

analysis

Writing R code is where AI helps most, and where a mistake is easiest to miss, because wrong code often runs without an error. Run the code yourself and look at what it filters: does it drop missing values, does it keep answers you meant to exclude? Then count one group by hand, for example with count(), and compare. If the numbers match, you can trust the rest a little more.

figures

An AI is good at the craft of a chart: sorting, labelling, colours. The request says what to change and that the data stay as they are. Check that the chart still shows the answer to your question, that bars start at zero, and that the labels say what you mean, in words your readers understand.

writing

For writing, ask for feedback rather than for text. A list of what is missing in your methods section is something you can check point by point, accept or reject. If you let it rewrite, read every sentence as if you had written it, because you will answer for it, and watch for claims it added without a source.
Why all this checking? Two studies show where AI goes wrong, and both found it in places where the answers looked convincing.

the jagged frontier

Dell'Acqua et al. (2023), navigating the jagged technological frontier, HBS working paper 24-013: 758 consultants, GPT-4
from the inside you cannot see where the frontier runs, so you check every answer, the convincing ones too.
In a field experiment at the Boston Consulting Group, 758 consultants worked on realistic tasks, some with GPT-4 and some without. On tasks the model handles well, inside the frontier, the group with AI finished 12.2 percent more tasks, was 25.1 percent faster and delivered work of more than 40 percent higher quality. On a task just outside the frontier, which looked similar, the group with AI was 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer than the group without. The frontier is jagged: tasks that look alike can lie on different sides of it, and nothing tells you on which side you are.

references that do not exist

Walters & Wilder (2023), Scientific Reports 13, 14045: 636 references in 84 short literature reviews
a reference that looks real is not a reference you have read.
Walters and Wilder had two models write short literature reviews on 42 topics and checked all 636 references. With GPT-3.5, 55 percent did not exist at all; with GPT-4 it was 18 percent. Of the references that did exist, many had wrong authors, years or pages. Newer models and agents that search the web make fewer such mistakes, but the problem has not gone away, and a made-up reference is one of the clearest signs that nobody checked.
an ai names a study that fits perfectly, with a doi. what do you do?
  1. cite it, the doi is there
  2. open it and read it
  3. ask the ai if it is real
  4. leave it out to be safe
Open it through the DOI and read it. A DOI can be made up as well, or lead to a different paper. Asking the AI whether the study is real tells you nothing, because it answers just as confidently either way. Leaving it out is safe but wastes a possibly good source.
Last part: three rules that hold whatever tool you use, and a simple way to document how you used it.

three rules

you answer for every sentence, also for what an ai wrote.
you write down what you used ai for and how you checked it.
nothing personal goes into the chat: no names, no free-text answers with personal details.
First: your name is on the paper, so every sentence, number and figure is yours, including those an AI produced. Second: say how you used AI, for which parts and how you checked the results; the next slide shows a simple table for it. Third: what you type or upload leaves your computer and goes to the provider's servers. Names of respondents, free-text answers with personal details and anything else that identifies a person stay out, and so do PDFs whose licence does not allow it.
