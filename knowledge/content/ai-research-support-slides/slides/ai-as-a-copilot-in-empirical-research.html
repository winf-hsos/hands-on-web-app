In a cockpit the copilot is fully qualified and flies large parts of the route, while the captain keeps the command and signs for the flight. That is the role an AI can take in your study: it can do a lot, often fast and well, but you set the course and you answer for what ends up in the paper.
First, what do we mean when we say AI? Three kinds, which differ in what they do and how much they do on their own.

three kinds of ai

Each click adds one kind. Chatbots answer your questions in a chat window: a question goes in, an answer comes out, and everything happens in the browser, apart from your own files. Specialized tools use AI for one specific job and often do it better than a chatbot: they find studies, read your PDFs with you, turn a text into a diagram or an outline into slides. Agents are different in kind: you give them a goal, not the way, and they reach it on their own in a loop. They plan, act and check what came out, and to act they use tools, above all your files, programs and the web. They keep going until the goal is reached; the next input explains how.

Where can these tools help? To answer that, we need the steps of a study first, and we take them from a standard textbook rather than inventing our own.

nine phases of a study

Döring (2023), Forschungsmethoden und Evaluation in den Sozial- und Humanwissenschaften, section 1.3.1 and chapters 5 to 13
Nicola Döring's textbook, the German standard for research methods in the social sciences, divides a quantitative study into nine phases and gives each one a chapter. You plan the study: a topic and a question, the state of research, and the study design. You collect data: you decide how to measure each concept, whom to ask, and you collect the answers. Then you prepare and analyze the data and present the results in a paper and a talk. The middle row only matters when you collect your own data; in a secondary analysis the people who ran the survey did it for you.
Now what is possible today. How we work with AI has changed in four levels, from a chatbot that answers questions to an AI that does everything on its own.

four levels of ai use

level 4: the reviewers of an ai proof in mathematics, checked in Lean: “we do not claim to fully understand the proof yet” (Science, 2026)
Four levels, one row per click, and each level adds something to the one above it. Every row shows the same places: your work, a folder; you; and on the right the chat or what comes out. Level 1, AI answers questions, as with the first chatbots: you ask in the chat, get an answer and carry it into your work by hand. Level 2, AI also writes documents: from a prompt in the chat it makes a draft of your methods section, a summary or a table, so it now makes content, but still in the chat and apart from your work, and you still carry it over. Level 3, AI also acts in your files: an agent works inside your folder with your files and programs. You give it a goal, not the way, it shows you results, you give feedback, and that goes round in a loop until the result fits. Level 4, AI also works on its own: the agent runs its own loop, you no longer appear, and the result goes out without anybody checking it, perhaps because nobody could follow it any more. In mathematics this has started: in November 2026, a committee chaired by the physicist David Gross reviewed an AI proof of the existence half of the Yang-Mills problem, which a computer had checked in the proof language Lean, and wrote that they do not claim to fully understand the proof yet. All four levels are possible today; whether they work and make sense is the question of the next part.

the four levels at a glance

each level adds something to the one below, and with every level you hand over more of the work and see less of it.
The same four levels as a table. On levels 1 and 2 the AI works in the chat, and you ask or prompt and carry the answer or the document into your work. On level 3 it works in your folder with tools, and your part is to set the goal and give feedback. On level 4 it works there too, and nobody checks the result. The last column says what each level adds: documents on level 2, tools, your files and a loop on level 3, and on level 4 that no human is in the loop any more. The higher the level, the more work the AI takes over, and the less of that work you see.

phases and levels

which level fits which phase, and which have you used yourself?
A task to think about with your neighbor: go through the nine phases and say which level of AI use fits, and where you have already used AI and at which level. Some ideas for the discussion. Research question: a chatbot can say which columns of the codebook fit your question, an agent can read codebook and data and test whether a question can be answered at all, but the more the AI picks, the more likely the question becomes the one that is easiest to answer. State of research: a specialized tool such as Consensus or Elicit finds and summarizes studies, an agent can search databases and draft the state of research, but only what you have opened and read goes into your paper. Data analysis: an agent writes, runs and fixes R code, and since wrong code often runs without an error, you check what it filters and count one group by hand. Presentation: a chatbot comments on your chart or text, an agent can draw all charts and even write the paper, and that is exactly where the question what makes sense is most pressing, because you answer for every sentence.
What is possible is not the same as what works. Studies show where AI goes wrong, and they found it in places where the answers looked convincing.

the jagged frontier

Dell'Acqua, Mollick et al. (2023), HBS working paper 24-013: 758 consultants, GPT-4; the term also in Mollick, Co-Existence (2026)
from the inside you cannot see where the frontier runs, so what looks possible still has to be checked.
In a field experiment at the Boston Consulting Group, 758 consultants worked on realistic tasks, some with GPT-4 and some without. On tasks inside the frontier of what the model handles well, the group with AI finished 12.2 percent more tasks, was 25.1 percent faster and delivered work of more than 40 percent higher quality. On a similar-looking task just outside the frontier, the group with AI was 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer. Ethan Mollick, one of the authors, coined the term jagged frontier for this and builds on it in his books Co-Intelligence and Co-Existence: AI is sometimes, but not always, better than you, and tasks that look alike can lie on different sides of the line. The frontier is the blue shape in the middle, and everything around it is outside. The yellow dot sits in a notch: a task that looks like those inside, but lies outside.

the frontier moves

a simplified picture of the idea, not a measurement; after Mollick, Co-Intelligence (2024) and Co-Existence (2026); 2027 as a hypothesis, as in Kokotajlo et al. (2025), AI 2027
the frontier grows and changes its shape, so with every new model you test again what lies inside.
A simplified picture of the idea, not a measurement. The frontier and the notch are the same as on the last slide, and around it are tasks from a study. In 2023 polishing a text and explaining an error message were inside. With every generation of models the frontier grows, but it does not simply get bigger: it changes its shape, some corners move far out, others shift sideways, and the notches move with them. In 2025 writing R code and summarizing a paper came inside, in 2026 running a whole analysis or searching the literature as an agent; yellow marks what has just come inside. Judging what matters in a study and citing only sources that really exist were still outside in 2026, as the next two slides show for the sources. The last step is a hypothesis, not a forecast: some, such as the authors of the scenario AI 2027 or the head of Anthropic, Dario Amodei, expect that around 2027 AI can do everything humans can, and better. Whether that comes true or not, the frontier keeps changing, and so the question is never only whether AI can do a task in general, but whether this task lies inside the frontier of the model you use right now.

references that do not exist

Walters & Wilder (2023), Scientific Reports 13, 14045: 636 references in 84 short literature reviews
a reference that looks real is not a reference you have read.
Walters and Wilder had two models write short literature reviews on 42 topics and checked all 636 references. With GPT-3.5, 55 percent did not exist at all; with GPT-4 it was 18 percent. Of the references that did exist, many had wrong authors, years or pages. These are the models of 2023, and for the newest models no comparable measurement has been published yet, so the next slide shows what we know from 2026.

made-up references in 2026

Topaz et al. (2026), The Lancet 407: 2.5 million papers · Rao et al. (2026): 221,000 cited links
made-up references still turn up, in ai answers and in published papers, so you open every one you cite.
Two studies from 2026. An audit of 2.5 million biomedical papers in The Lancet checked their references against PubMed, Crossref and other databases: in 2023, one paper in 2,828 cited a reference that does not exist, in 2025 one in 458, and in the first seven weeks of 2026 one in 277. The authors cannot prove that AI wrote these references, but the rise follows the spread of chatbots. A second study checked more than 200,000 links that AI systems cited as sources: of the links from two deep research agents, 10.7 percent led nowhere and had never existed, from eight models with web search 4.8 percent. These two numbers come from systems of 2025 such as GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet and Gemini 2.5; for GPT-5.1 and Claude Sonnet 4.5 the same study found 8 to 9 percent of cited links that did not resolve, without separating made-up links from merely dead ones. For the very newest models there are no such numbers yet. The models got better, but the problem did not go away, and a deep research mode that cites more sources also invents more of them.
Last part: three rules that hold whatever tool and whatever level you choose, and a simple way to document how you used AI.

three rules

you answer for every sentence, also for what an ai wrote.
you write down what you used ai for and how you checked it.
you watch for bias: ai repeats what is frequent in its data, not what is true for the people you study.
First: your name is on the paper, so every sentence, number and figure is yours, including those an AI produced, whatever the level. Second: say how you used AI, for which parts and how you checked the results; the next slide shows a simple table for it. Third: an AI learned from texts in which some groups, languages and views are frequent and others are rare, and it repeats that picture. A summary of studies, a coding of free-text answers or a description of a group can therefore be skewed, so you ask whom a result might leave out and compare it with your data. Fairness is one of the core requirements in the European Commission's ethics guidelines for trustworthy AI.

write down how you used ai

One row per use: which tool with which model, what for, in which section, and how you checked the result. The last column is the one that counts, because it shows that you stayed the author. Such a table fits in an appendix of your paper and takes a minute per row if you fill it in as you go.

That is the message to take home. What is possible changes with every model, and no study can tell you where the frontier runs for your task with your tool. You find that out only by using AI yourself, checking what comes out and keeping what works. Nobody has the full map yet, as the picture shows: all of us, researchers, teachers and students, are exploring the jagged frontier, and every use that works draws the coastline a little further out.
