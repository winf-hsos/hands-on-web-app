In a cockpit the copilot is fully qualified and flies large parts of the route, while the captain keeps the command and signs for the flight. That is the role an AI can take in your study: it can do a lot, often fast and well, but you set the course and you answer for what ends up in the paper.
First, what do we mean when we say AI? Three kinds of tools, which differ in how broad they are and what they can reach.

three kinds of ai

selection after the overview of ai tools of the university library Duisburg-Essen, plus HAWKI
Chatbots such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Chat AI, the free and GDPR-compliant service of the GWDG for German universities, or HAWKI, the chat interface of Osnabrück University of Applied Sciences, answer in a chat on almost any topic, and many of them can now also search the web or run a deep research. They work with what you type or upload. Research tools do one job in research and often do it better than a chatbot: Consensus and Elicit search scientific papers and summarize them, SciSpace helps you read them, Research Rabbit and Litmaps draw networks of citations, and NotebookLM answers questions about the PDFs you give it. They work with their database of papers and with your PDFs. Agents are as broad as chatbots but act on their own: OpenCode, Claude Code or Codex read and change your files, run programs and search the web until a task is done; the next input explains how. The small drawing in each column shows how the kind works: a chat with questions and answers, a search in a database of papers, and an agent that works with your files, programs and the web in a loop. Every tool name is a link. The library of the University of Duisburg-Essen keeps an overview of such tools, and the selection here follows it.
Where can these tools help? To answer that, we need the steps of a study first, and we take them from a standard textbook rather than inventing our own.

nine phases of a study

Döring (2023), Forschungsmethoden und Evaluation in den Sozial- und Humanwissenschaften, section 1.3.1 and chapters 5 to 13
Nicola Döring's textbook, the German standard for research methods in the social sciences, divides a quantitative study into nine phases and gives each one a chapter. You plan the study: a topic and a question, the state of research, and the study design. You collect data: you decide how to measure each concept, whom to ask, and you collect the answers. Then you prepare and analyze the data and present the results in a paper and a talk. In a secondary analysis, as with the survey in this course, the middle row was done by the people who ran the survey; you inherit their questionnaire, sample and data, and the codebook tells you how. Your work lies in the first and the last row.
Now what is possible today, phase by phase. For each phase there are three levels of AI use, from an AI that only advises to an agent that does the whole step.

three levels of ai use

Each row is one level, and time runs from left to right: white bars are your work, blue bars the work of the AI, gray bars the tools an agent uses, and the green bar is the moment you check a result. On the first level you do the work yourself and ask the AI in between, much like a tutor. On the second level you give the AI a task, it makes a draft, and you review it. On the third level you give an agent a goal, and it works on its own, uses tools and stays in its loop until the goal is reached; then you check the result. Each click plays one level. All three levels are possible today; whether they work and make sense is the question of the next part.

research question

With the codebook at hand, an AI can say which columns fit your question, suggest questions the data can answer, or as an agent read codebook and data itself and test whether a question can be answered at all. The risk grows with the level: the more the AI picks, the more likely the question becomes the one that is easiest to answer rather than the one you wanted to ask.

literature

On the first level a chatbot suggests search terms and you search the library catalog and a scholarly database yourself. On the second, a research tool such as Consensus or Elicit finds studies and summarizes them, and you read the best ones. On the third, an agent searches the databases itself, if need be by steering a browser, reads the papers and writes a draft of the state of research. Whatever the level, only what you have opened and read goes into your paper.

analysis

You can write the R code yourself and ask an AI to explain an error, let it write the code and run it yourself, or let an agent write, run and fix the code and report the results. Wrong code often runs without an error, so at every level you look at what the code filters and how it treats missing values, and you count one group by hand.

figures

An AI is good at the craft of a chart: sorting, labeling, colors. It can comment on your chart, write the ggplot2 code for you, or as an agent draw all charts and put them into the paper. You check that each chart still answers your question, that bars start at zero and that the labels are in your words.

writing

An AI can give feedback on your text, draft a section from your notes, or as an agent write the whole paper from data and sources. The third level is possible today, and it is exactly where the question "what makes sense?" is most pressing: a paper is the record of your thinking, and you answer for every sentence in it.
What is possible is not the same as what works. Studies show where AI goes wrong, and they found it in places where the answers looked convincing.

the jagged frontier

Dell'Acqua, Mollick et al. (2023), HBS working paper 24-013: 758 consultants, GPT-4; the term also in Mollick, Co-Existence (2026)
from the inside you cannot see where the frontier runs, so what looks possible still has to be checked.
In a field experiment at the Boston Consulting Group, 758 consultants worked on realistic tasks, some with GPT-4 and some without. On tasks inside the frontier of what the model handles well, the group with AI finished 12.2 percent more tasks, was 25.1 percent faster and delivered work of more than 40 percent higher quality. On a similar-looking task just outside the frontier, the group with AI was 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer. Ethan Mollick, one of the authors, coined the term jagged frontier for this and builds on it in his books Co-Intelligence and Co-Existence: AI is sometimes, but not always, better than you, and tasks that look alike can lie on different sides of the line. Drawn as a closed shape, the frontier has an inside and an outside, and the yellow dot sits in a notch: a task that looks like those inside, but lies outside.

the frontier moves

a simplified picture of the idea, not a measurement; after Mollick, Co-Intelligence (2024) and Co-Existence (2026)
the frontier keeps moving out, but it stays jagged, so a task can lie outside however easy it looks.
A simplified picture of the idea, not a measurement. With every generation of models the frontier moves out and takes in tasks that were outside before: polishing a text and explaining an error message early on, then writing R code and summarizing a paper, then running a whole analysis or searching the literature as an agent. Yellow marks what has just come inside. But the shape stays jagged, and some tasks remain in a notch however far the frontier grows: citing only sources that really exist, as the next two slides show, and judging what matters in a study, which is your job anyway. So the question is never only whether AI can do a task in general, but whether this task lies inside the frontier of the model you use right now.

references that do not exist

Walters & Wilder (2023), Scientific Reports 13, 14045: 636 references in 84 short literature reviews
a reference that looks real is not a reference you have read.
Walters and Wilder had two models write short literature reviews on 42 topics and checked all 636 references. With GPT-3.5, 55 percent did not exist at all; with GPT-4 it was 18 percent. Of the references that did exist, many had wrong authors, years or pages. These are the models of 2023, and for the newest models no comparable measurement has been published yet, so the next slide shows what we know from 2026.

made-up references in 2026

Topaz et al. (2026), The Lancet 407: 2.5 million papers · Rao et al. (2026): 221,000 cited links
made-up references still turn up, in ai answers and in published papers, so you open every one you cite.
Two studies from 2026. An audit of 2.5 million biomedical papers in The Lancet checked their references against PubMed, Crossref and other databases: in 2023, one paper in 2,828 cited a reference that does not exist, in 2025 one in 458, and in the first seven weeks of 2026 one in 277. The authors cannot prove that AI wrote these references, but the rise follows the spread of chatbots. A second study checked more than 200,000 links that AI systems cited as sources: of the links from two deep research agents, 10.7 percent led nowhere and had never existed, from eight models with web search 4.8 percent. These two numbers come from systems of 2025 such as GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet and Gemini 2.5; for GPT-5.1 and Claude Sonnet 4.5 the same study found 8 to 9 percent of cited links that did not resolve, without separating made-up links from merely dead ones. For the very newest models there are no such numbers yet. The models got better, but the problem did not go away, and a deep research mode that cites more sources also invents more of them.
Last part: three rules that hold whatever tool and whatever level you choose, and a simple way to document how you used AI.

three rules

you answer for every sentence, also for what an ai wrote.
you write down what you used ai for and how you checked it.
you watch for bias: ai repeats what is frequent in its data, not what is true for the people you study.
First: your name is on the paper, so every sentence, number and figure is yours, including those an AI produced, whatever the level. Second: say how you used AI, for which parts and how you checked the results; the next slide shows a simple table for it. Third: an AI learned from texts in which some groups, languages and views are frequent and others are rare, and it repeats that picture. A summary of studies, a coding of free-text answers or a description of a group can therefore be skewed, so you ask whom a result might leave out and compare it with your data. Fairness is one of the core requirements in the European Commission's ethics guidelines for trustworthy AI.

write down how you used ai

One row per use: which tool with which model, what for, in which section, and how you checked the result. The last column is the one that counts, because it shows that you stayed the author. Such a table fits in an appendix of your paper and takes a minute per row if you fill it in as you go.
