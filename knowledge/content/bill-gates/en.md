## Who he is

William Henry Gates was born in Seattle in 1955 and founded Microsoft in 1975
together with his school friend Paul Allen. Few people have shaped as much as
he did which software ran on personal computers in the decades that followed.
For anyone learning to program, the interesting part of his story is less the
company and the fortune than three ideas from the early years.

## A program for a machine you do not have

In early 1975 the Altair 8800 appeared on the cover of an electronics
magazine: a kit computer without keyboard or screen, operated with toggle
switches. Gates, then a student at Harvard, and Allen offered the manufacturer
a programming language for it, an interpreter for BASIC. They did not own an
Altair. So Allen wrote a program that imitated the Altair's processor, the
Intel 8080, on one of the university's mainframes, and the two developed and
tested their BASIC on this emulator. When Allen started it on the real machine
at the manufacturer for the first time, it ran, as the story goes, at the first
attempt.

The idea behind it is everyday practice today: if you describe a machine
precisely enough, you can rebuild it in software and write programs for it
before the hardware is on your desk.

## Software costs money

In 1976 Gates wrote an open letter to the computer hobbyists of his time. Many
of them simply copied Altair BASIC instead of buying it, and Gates argued that
good software takes a lot of work and that nobody would write it without being
paid. At the time this was an outsider's view, because programs were seen as
an extra that came with the hardware. The question whether software is
something you buy or something you share has not gone away since, and the
free and open source movement is also an answer to this position.

## The layer between program and machine

In 1980 IBM was looking for an operating system for its first personal
computer. Microsoft bought the system of a small company in Seattle, adapted
it and delivered it as DOS. The contract was what mattered: Microsoft kept the
right to sell the same system to other manufacturers as well. When many
companies soon built computers compatible with the IBM PC, almost all of them
ran MS-DOS and later Windows.

An operating system is a layer between programs and hardware. Programs talk to
this layer instead of to the device directly, which is why the same program
runs on computers from different manufacturers. Whoever provides this layer
has a say in what is possible above it. That was exactly the issue in a major
antitrust case of the US government against Microsoft in the late 1990s.

## Later

Gates led Microsoft as chief executive until 2000 and withdrew from day-to-day
business in 2008. Since then he has worked mainly for his foundation, which is
concerned with health and poverty around the world.

## Key points

- An emulator imitates a machine in software. That way you can program for
  hardware you do not have.
- Whether software is bought or freely shared was never a given and is still a
  decision today.
- An operating system is a layer between program and machine, and whoever
  provides it has great influence on everything above it.
