This input is about tidy data: the shape a table needs so that the verbs of dplyr can answer a question with it, and the verbs of tidyr that bring a messy table into that shape. The message to take home is that a table is tidy when every variable has its column, every observation its row and every value its cell, and that four verbs bring almost any messy table there.
Five parts: what tidy means, how a wide table becomes long, how a long table becomes wide, what to do when one cell holds more than one thing, and a map from what you see in a table to the verb that repairs it.

First the idea, without code: what makes a table tidy, and why that matters for every analysis that follows.

one answer, four tables

all four hold the same numbers, but only the first lets you ask a question with the verbs you know.
Three respondents of the survey said which price for a litre of milk they find fair, for three brands: Weihenstephan, Gut & Günstig and alpro. These are their real answers, written down four times, and each card says in gray how its table is built. The table on the left has one row per answer, with the columns respondent, brand and price, and because respondent identifies the person, it repeats once for every brand that person judged. On the key, the second: one column per brand and one row per person. Next key, the third: all prices of a person in one cell. Next key, the fourth: one column per person and the brands as rows. None of the four is wrong, and people find the second one the easiest to read. Last key: whoever wants the median per brand gets it from the first table without any rebuilding, with group_by(brand) and summarize(), while the second would need three calculations and the third would first have to cut text apart.

three rules

These are the three rules of tidy data as Hadley Wickham, who also wrote dplyr and tidyr, put them, shown on the first table. On the key, the column brand: every variable has its own column, and a variable is something that is measured or asked, such as the brand or the price. Next key, one row: every observation has its own row, and an observation is everything measured about one unit, here one person judging one brand, respondent 16 on alpro. Last key, one cell: every value has its own cell, a single statement such as 1.49.

what is one observation?

what counts as one observation depends on the question, so tidy is a property of a table and a question together.
Two tables with the same three people. On the key, the question how old the people are who answered: here the unit is the person, one row per person is exactly right, and the wide table on the left is tidy for this question. Next key, the question which brand people think should be cheapest: now the unit is one person judging one brand, three rows belong to every person, and the long table on the right is the tidy one. Whether a table is tidy cannot be decided without the question.

why it pays off

in a tidy table one line of code covers every brand, however many there are.
Three medians, once from each table. In the wide table every brand needs its own line, and with thirty brands that would be thirty lines and thirty chances to mistype a name. In the long table one group_by() and one summarize() cover every brand, however many there are. ggplot2 works the same way, because it can only colour by brand when the brand is a column.
respondents gave a price for each of three brands, one column per brand. is this table tidy?
  1. yes, one row per person
  2. no, values in column names
  3. no, two values in one cell
  4. it depends on the question
Quick quiz. The intended answer is that values sit in the column names: weihen, gut and alpro are values of the variable brand. Strictly speaking the last answer is right as well, and that is the bridge to the slide on observations: for every question that compares brands, the brands hide in the column names, while for a question about each person the table is fine as it is.

four ways to be messy

This is the map for the rest of the input: four ways in which a table can be messy, each shown on a small piece of our own data with the messy part in yellow and the verb that repairs it underneath. When the brands stand in the column names, pivot_longer() repairs it; when one column called measure holds both the price and the certainty, pivot_wider(); when a cell holds a range such as 1.30 to 1.50, separate_wider_delim(); and when a cell holds a list of shops, separate_longer_delim(). All four come from the package tidyr, which library(tidyverse) loads together with dplyr.
Values in column names are the most common kind of mess, and pivot_longer() repairs it.

the raw survey

library(tidyverse) raw <- read_csv( "https://hands-on-computer-science.de/media/datasets/mds12_schoko_milch.csv" ) |> mutate(respondent = row_number(), .before = 1)

From here on we work with the raw version of the survey on milk and chocolate, as it came out of the survey tool: 2,811 rows, one per respondent, and 606 columns. read_csv() loads it straight from the site. The file has no column that tells the respondents apart, so mutate() with row_number() numbers the rows in a new column respondent, and .before = 1 puts it first. the price question, as the survey stores it

the brand is a variable, but here it hides in three column names.
The question which price for a litre is fair, asked for three brands, is stored in three columns: u013pzahl_1weihen, u013pzahl_2gut and u013pzahl_3alpro. The first row holds the answers we have seen, 1.49, 1.09 and 1.89, rows 2 and 3 are empty, and further down respondents 16 and 36 answered again, while in all 2,580 of the 2,811 rows are empty, because only 231 people were asked this question. The brand is a variable, but it hides in the column names.

pivot_longer(): columns become rows

The three respondents again, with simple column names. First key: the three brand columns are the ones to turn into rows, while respondent stays as it is. Second key: the first row unfolds. Its respondent number is copied three times, the three column names travel as values into a new column brand, and the three prices into a column price, so that three rows stand on the right. Third key: the other two respondents follow at once, and the long table has nine rows. Last key: the wide table goes, and above the long one appear the two arguments that name the new columns, names_to for the column that takes the names and values_to for the one that takes the values.

the code

prices <- tribble( ~respondent, ~weihen, ~gut, ~alpro, 1, 1.49, 1.09, 1.89, 16, 1.19, 1.19, 1.49, 36, 1.09, 1.09, 1.49 ) prices |> pivot_longer( cols = c(weihen, gut, alpro), names_to = "brand", values_to = "price" )

The same in R. tribble() writes a small table row by row, so the block runs as it is: live moment, copy it into an R box or into Positron and run it. pivot_longer() needs three answers: which columns, in cols; where their names go, in names_to; and where their values go, in values_to. The two new names are in quotes, because these columns do not exist yet. The result is the long table with nine rows.

cleaning up on the way

Two more arguments clean up while pivoting. names_pattern takes from each column name only the part inside the brackets: \\d stands for the digit before it and (.*) for everything after it, so u013pzahl_1weihen becomes weihen. values_drop_na = TRUE drops the empty rows. On the key, the result: 693 rows remain, three for each of the 231 respondents, and brand reads weihen, gut and alpro. Whoever does not like regular expressions writes names_prefix = "u013pzahl_" instead and keeps the digit, as in 1weihen; names_sep, which splits a name at a character, comes up again in part 3. We keep the result under the name prices_long.

nine columns of zeros and ones

a question with several answers is one variable, however many columns the survey tool makes of it.
Where people shop for groceries was a question with several answers allowed: discounter, supermarket, hypermarket, specialist shop and five more. The survey tool stores such a question as nine columns, one per shop, with 1 for ticked and 0 for not ticked; the drawing shows the first four of them as R prints them, with the yellow ones ticked. Survey tools almost always store multiple choice like this, but where someone shops is one variable, however many columns it fills.

The opposite case: a table that is too long, because one column holds more than one variable.

two variables in one column

price and certainty are two variables, so they need two columns, even if the table looks tidy at first sight.
A small long table, made up for this example: two respondents, two brands, and in the column measure two different things, the price someone finds fair and how sure they are to buy the brand, on a scale from -4 to 4. Data from a database or a measuring device often look like this. At first sight the table looks tidy, with one value per row, but the mean of value would be nonsense, because it mixes euros and points on a scale. Price and certainty are two variables, so they need two columns, and pivot_wider() gives them those.

pivot_wider(): rows become columns

The table from before. First key: measure and value are the columns that turn into new columns. Second key: the two rows of respondent 1 on weihen travel together into one row; price and sure rise up as the names of two new columns, and their values move beneath them, while the rows that are done turn gray. Third key: all other pairs follow at once, and on the right stand respondent, brand, price and sure, one row per person and brand.

the code

answers <- tribble( ~respondent, ~brand, ~measure, ~value, 1, "weihen", "price", 1.49, 1, "weihen", "sure", 2, 1, "alpro", "price", 1.89, 1, "alpro", "sure", -1, 16, "weihen", "price", 1.19, 16, "weihen", "sure", 3, 16, "alpro", "price", 1.49, 16, "alpro", "sure", 1 ) answers |> pivot_wider( names_from = measure, values_from = value )

The same in R, with the small table written by tribble(), so that the block runs as it is. pivot_wider() needs two answers: names_from, the column that supplies the names of the new columns, and values_from, the column that fills them. Both are bare column names without quotes, because they name columns that already exist, while names_to and values_to in pivot_longer() name columns that do not exist yet.

two directions

The two verbs undo each other: pivot_longer() turns columns into rows, and pivot_wider() turns rows back into columns. Whoever is unsure which direction they need asks what one row should be: one person, or one person and one brand.

two variables across two brands

A harder case: two variables, price and sure, each spread over the brands, in four columns. First key: every column name splits at the underscore into two parts, the first, in yellow, naming a variable and the second a brand. Second key: the yellow parts become the names of columns, the other parts become values in a column brand, and the result is the tidy table with respondent, brand, price and sure, the same as after pivot_wider().

names_to with ".value"

both <- tribble( ~respondent, ~price_weihen, ~price_alpro, ~sure_weihen, ~sure_alpro, 1, 1.49, 1.89, 2, -1, 16, 1.19, 1.49, 3, 1 ) both |> pivot_longer( cols = -respondent, names_to = c(".value", "brand"), names_sep = "_" )
the result is the tidy table with the columns respondent, brand, price and sure
cols = -respondent takes every column except respondent. names_sep = "_" splits every column name at the underscore, and names_to gets two names for the two parts. The special name ".value" says that this part of the name stays a column name, while the second part goes into a new column brand. So one pivot_longer() turns the wide table into the tidy one in a single step, without a pivot_wider() afterwards.
The last kind of mess sits inside the cells: one cell that holds two values, or a whole list.

two values in one cell

A price range stored as text, the way a questionnaire offers it to choose from; the small table is made up for this example. First key: in every cell the separator " to " lights up. Second key: the cell splits there, and the parts on its left and right move into two columns of their own, from and to.

separate_wider_delim()

ranges <- tribble( ~brand, ~range, "weihen", "1.30 to 1.50", "gut", "0.90 to 1.10", "alpro", "1.50 to 1.70" ) ranges |> separate_wider_delim( range, delim = " to ", names = c("from", "to") ) |> mutate(from = as.numeric(from), to = as.numeric(to))
separate_wider_delim() takes the column to split, the separator in delim and the names of the new columns in names. What is cut out of a text stays text, so as.numeric() turns from and to into numbers. The opposite, which joins two columns into one, is called unite().

Where three respondents shop, written as a list in one cell, the way some survey tools and many spreadsheets store it. The shops are the real answers of respondents 12, 24 and 48, translated into English. First key: the commas light up. Second key: every cell falls apart at its commas, the parts slide below each other, and the respondent number is copied for every new row, so that seven rows stand on the right, one per person and shop.

separate_longer_delim()

shopping <- tribble( ~respondent, ~shops, 12, "supermarket", 24, "discounter, supermarket, farm shop", 48, "supermarket, specialist shop, organic shop" ) shopping |> separate_longer_delim(shops, delim = ", ") |> count(shops)

wider splits a cell into columns, longer splits it into rows, and the question decides which one you need.
separate_longer_delim() splits at the comma and the space after it and puts every part into a row of its own, and after that count() works as on any tidy column. On the key, the result: all three people shop at the supermarket, and every other shop is named once. A price range has a fixed number of parts and becomes columns, while a list has as many parts as someone ticked and becomes rows.

spreadsheet habits

in a spreadsheet a group is often named only once, at the top of its block, because the eye fills in the rest.
In spreadsheets a group is often named only in the first row of its block: the brand stands at the top, and the rows below are left empty because a reader fills them in without thinking. For R the empty cells are missing values, which read_csv() and read_excel() read as NA, and a filter on alpro would find only one of the three rows.

fill()

sheet <- tribble( ~brand, ~respondent, ~price, "weihen", 1, 1.49, NA, 16, 1.19, NA, 36, 1.09, "alpro", 1, 1.89, NA, 16, 1.49, NA, 36, 1.49 ) sheet |> fill(brand)
The same table in R, where the empty cells are NA. fill() writes the last known value downward into the missing cells, and on the key the gaps fill, block by block. Related is drop_na(), which removes rows with missing values, but use it only once you know why they are missing.
