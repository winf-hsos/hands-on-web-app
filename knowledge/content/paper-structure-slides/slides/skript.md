# How a scientific paper is built

Script to the deck `how-a-scientific-paper-is-built.html`, in the order of the
slides. Frame numbers count every build-up step, as in the PDF.

The message of this input fits into one sentence: every section of a paper
has one job, and the most common mistake is doing the job of another section,
above all interpreting in the results. It applies to journal articles and
equally to a student paper or a thesis that analyses data.

## Opening (frames 1–3)

Readers don't read a paper from start to end. They read the title and the
abstract first, then often the discussion, and only after that, if at all,
the methods. They can jump like this only because almost all empirical papers
are built the same way, like houses that all follow one floor plan, so that
you know where the kitchen is before you open the door. Every room therefore
has to be where readers expect it.

## Part 1: one shape for all papers (frames 4–14)

**imrad (frames 5–8).** A paper is a house with four rooms in a row, and each
room answers one question: the introduction says why the study matters and
ends with the question, the methods say how you found out, the results say
what you found, and the discussion says what it means. Title and abstract are
the front door, the references the small room at the back. Döring (2023,
p. 770) names the state of research as a room of its own between introduction
and methods; many international journals fold it into the introduction, which
then holds the background, the state of research and the questions or
hypotheses.

**How imrad won (frame 9).** Sollaci and Pereira (2004) counted, every five
years from 1935 to 1985, how many of 1,297 original articles in four leading
medical journals (BMJ, JAMA, The Lancet and the New England Journal of
Medicine) used the four headings. Their text reports no such article in 1935,
more than ten percent in 1950, about twenty percent in 1955, more than eighty
percent in the 1970s, and in the 1980s it was the only form left. The slide
shows these stated values as dots, joined by a line, since the paper's own
figure is not reproduced; on the 100 percent line it marks the years from
which each journal printed nothing but imrad: the New England Journal of
Medicine from 1975, the BMJ from 1980, JAMA and The Lancet from 1985. The
structure had been recommended since the 1920s, but authors used it only once
editors insisted on it after the Second World War. The reason the authors
give is modular reading: readers browse a paper section by section and look
for each piece of information where they expect it.

**How long is each part? (frames 10 and 11).** Döring (2023, p. 771) gives a
rule of thumb for an empirical paper of twenty pages: one page of
introduction, four of theory, state of research and research questions, two
of methods, six of results, three of discussion and four of references. The
results take the most room, and a paper of twenty pages that spends twelve on
theories and only two on results is, in her word, unbalanced; the slide shows
such a paper below the rule of thumb, with the six remaining pages undivided,
since the book gives only these two numbers, and marks it with a small warning
sign as the one not to follow, while a check marks the rule of thumb. In a shorter student paper the
pages shrink, but the proportions stay.

**The hourglass (frames 12–14).** A paper has the shape of an hourglass
(after Cals and Kotz, 2013). Its width shows how broad the text is, not how
long a part is: at the rims it speaks about the whole field, in the neck only
about your own study, and the slide says so on the left. The introduction
starts broad, with the topic and
what is known about it, and narrows to the gap and to your question. Methods
and results stay narrow, because they are about your study only. The
discussion widens again, from the answer to the comparison with other
studies, the limitations and what follows from it. The introduction ends with
your question and the discussion begins with its answer, so whoever reads only
the last paragraph of the one and the first paragraph of the other knows what
the paper is about.

## Part 2: what goes where (frames 15–28)

**Moving boxes (frame 16).** Every sentence of a paper belongs in one room,
just as every moving box carries the name of the room it goes to. The four
slides that follow take a short paper on the milk survey apart, written for
this input, with the question whether price limits for milk differ by diet.
Each slide shows the building blocks of one section, one panel per block:
first the sentences, then the name of each block. The stack of panels has
the shape of the section, a funnel for the introduction, a list for methods
and results and an upside-down funnel for the discussion. Below the panels,
quietly, stands a sentence that would be out of place there, with the room it
belongs in.

**Introduction (frames 17–19).** The introduction is a funnel, and its four
blocks narrow like one: "milk competes on price in every supermarket. Earlier studies
asked why people choose plant-based milk (Dorina et al., 2025), but whether
price limits differ with diet is not known. Do price limits for milk differ by
diet?" It moves from the topic to what is known, to the gap and, in its last
paragraph, to the question. "Vegetarians accept higher prices" stays out: it
is a finding and belongs in the results, and in the introduction it would give
away the answer before the question is asked.

**Methods (frames 20–22).** The methods are a recipe that would let someone
else repeat the analysis: "we reanalysed the milk and chocolate survey, an
existing online survey of 2,809 adults in Germany. 619 respondents saw one of
two milks and were asked from which price it would be too expensive; 611 named
one. We left out 23 prices below 0.50 € or above 10 €, which can hardly be
meant for a litre, so 588 remained. We compared the median price limit of the
diet groups." The page names the data source and says that the data are
reused, then the measure and the sample, the cleaning and the analysis; the
Journal Article Reporting Standards of the APA (JARS-Quant, 2020) ask for
exactly these points, including the note that a study is a secondary
analysis. The cleaning is real: 20 of the 611 prices lie above 10 euros, up to
999, and 3 below 50 cents. "The median is the middle value of a sorted list"
stays out: a paper names the median instead of explaining it (Döring, 2023,
p. 771), so the sentence belongs in a textbook.

**Results (frames 23–25).** "Vegetarians named a median price limit of
2.45 € (n = 28), omnivores one of 1.99 € (n = 362). Figure 1 shows all five
diet groups." The text names the finding the question is about, with the size
of each group, and points to the figure; the figure adds the other three
groups (flexitarians and pescetarians 1.99 €, vegans 2.00 €) instead of
repeating the text, since each finding appears once (Döring, 2023, p. 772).
"Probably because they value animal welfare" stays out: it says why, and why
belongs in the discussion.

**Discussion (frames 26–28).** The discussion opens with the answer and then
widens like an upside-down funnel: "only vegetarians named higher price
limits. Whether this reflects concern for animals, the data cannot say. Only 28
vegetarians named a limit, and each respondent saw only one of two milks. A
larger sample should test whether the difference holds." The answer comes
first (Döring, 2023, p. 770), then what the data cannot say, the limitations
and what follows. "Women named higher price limits than men" stays out: it is
true in this survey (2.00 € against 1.89 €), but it is a new finding, and a
finding that appears for the first time in the discussion belongs in the
results.

## Part 3: the most common mistake (frames 29–40)

**A bathtub in the kitchen (frame 30).** The mistake that appears most often
is not a wrong number but a sentence standing in the wrong room. A bathtub is
a good bathtub, but in the kitchen it is in the way; an interpretation is a
good sentence in the discussion and a mistake in the results.

**Quick quiz (frames 31–33, live poll `paper-sentence-section`).** Where does
this sentence belong: "vegetarians named a higher price limit, probably
because they value animal welfare"? As it stands, in the discussion, because
its second half interprets. Its first half, however, is a finding, and
findings belong in the results.

**Result or interpretation (frames 34–36).** The sentence holds two
statements, and the slide cuts it in two. The first half goes into the results
and becomes a whole sentence with its numbers: vegetarians named a higher
price limit than omnivores, a median of 2.45 euros against 1.99 euros
(n = 28 and 362). The second half goes into the discussion and becomes a
cautious sentence: this may reflect that they value animal welfare, a reason
the data themselves do not show.

**Dissecting a real paper (frames 37–40).** The outline is that of an article
on plant-based milk by Dorina, Nikpour, Mullan and Uren (2025, *Foods* 14(11),
1970, CC BY 4.0), an online survey of 286 people that follows imrad
throughout. The three marks on the outline are what to read first, and
the numbers give the order, which the slide also says in a note. Read every
paper in this order: first the question at the end of
the introduction, here section 1.2, "the current study", with its three
hypotheses; then the answer at the start of the discussion, whose first
paragraph says which hypotheses were supported; then the limitations, here
the subsection "strengths and limitations", before trusting the answer. Only
then check whether the methods carry it. Whoever reads the results closely
will also find a number in them that cannot be right.

## Part 4: the abstract (frames 41–47)

**A property listing (frame 42).** The abstract is the paper's listing: one
paragraph that tells a reader whether to open the door. Most readers see only
the title and the abstract and decide from them whether to read on.

**The paper in miniature (frames 43–46).** The abstract of the example paper
takes one sentence from each room, in the order of the rooms, each sentence
underlined in its own colour (after Döring,
2023, p. 776): "Milk competes on price, but whether price limits for milk
differ by diet has not been asked. We reanalysed an online survey of 2,809
adults in Germany and compared, across five diets, the price from which 588
of them found a milk too expensive. Vegetarians named a median limit of
2.45 € (n = 28), the other four diets 1.99 € or 2.00 €. Price limits may be
higher among vegetarians, which a larger sample should test." It has 72 words.
It is written last, when the findings are known, and journals usually allow
between 150 and 250 words; the APA Publication Manual (2020) sets 250 as the
upper limit.

**Closing (frame 47).** The floor plan from the beginning now has its house.
Write your question at the end of the introduction and your answer at the
start of the discussion, and let everything else connect the two. With this
structure you can set up the template of your own paper: the headings are
already there, and you fill the rooms.
