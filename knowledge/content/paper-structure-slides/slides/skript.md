# How a scientific paper is built

Script to the deck `how-a-scientific-paper-is-built.html`, in the order of the
slides. Frame numbers count every build-up step, as in the PDF.

The message of this input fits into one sentence: every section of a paper
has one job, and the most common mistake is doing the job of another section,
above all interpreting in the results. It applies to journal articles and
equally to a student paper or a thesis that analyses data.

## Opening (frames 1–3)

Readers don't read a paper from start to end. They read the title and the
abstract first, then often the discussion, and only after that, if at all,
the methods. They can jump like this only because almost all empirical papers
are built the same way, like houses that all follow one floor plan, so that
you know where the kitchen is before you open the door. Every room therefore
has to be where readers expect it.

## Part 1: one shape for all papers (frames 4–13)

**imrad.** An empirical paper has four sections, and each answers one
question: the introduction says why the study matters and ends with the
question, the methods say how you found out, the results say what you found,
and the discussion says what it means. Title and abstract stand before them,
the references after them. Döring (2023, p. 770) names the state of research as
a fifth section between introduction and methods; many international journals
fold it into the introduction, which then holds the background, the state of
research and the questions or hypotheses.

**How imrad won.** Sollaci and Pereira (2004) counted, every five years from
1935 to 1985, how many of 1,297 original articles in four leading medical
journals (BMJ, JAMA, The Lancet and the New England Journal of Medicine) used
the four headings. Their text reports no such article in 1935, more than ten
percent in 1950, about twenty percent in 1955, more than eighty percent in the
1970s, and in the 1980s it was the only form left. The slide shows these
stated values as dots; the dashed line only connects them, since the paper's
own figure is not reproduced. The structure had been recommended since the
1920s, but authors used it only once editors insisted on it after the Second
World War. The reason the authors give is modular reading: readers browse a
paper section by section and look for each piece of information where they
expect it.

**How long is each part?** Döring (2023, p. 771) gives a rule of thumb for an
empirical paper of twenty pages: one page of introduction, four of theory,
state of research and research questions, two of methods, six of results,
three of discussion and four of references. The results take the most room,
and a paper of twenty pages that spends twelve on theories and only two on
results is, in her word, unbalanced. In a shorter student paper the pages
shrink, but the proportions stay.

**The hourglass.** A paper has the shape of an hourglass (after Cals and
Kotz, 2013). The introduction starts broad, with the topic and what is known
about it, and narrows to the gap and to your question. Methods and results
stay narrow, because they are about your study only. The discussion widens
again, from the answer to the comparison with other studies, the limitations
and what follows from it. The introduction ends with your question and the
discussion begins with its answer, so whoever reads only the last paragraph
of the one and the first paragraph of the other knows what the paper is about.

## Part 2: what goes where (frames 14–18)

**Introduction.** The introduction is a funnel: from the general topic to
what is known, from there to what is not known yet, and in its last paragraph
to the question of the study. What stays out is a summary of everything ever
written on the topic, because only what leads to the question belongs here,
and the results. A question that readers meet for the first time in the
results or the discussion shows that the introduction did not do its job.

**Methods.** The methods are a recipe that would let someone else repeat the
analysis. For an analysis of an existing survey this means saying openly that
you reuse data someone else collected and who collected them, who was asked
and how they were recruited, which questions you use in their wording, how
you cleaned the data (for example which implausible values you set to
missing) and how you analysed them. The Journal Article Reporting Standards
of the APA (JARS-Quant, 2020) ask for exactly this, including the note that a
study is a secondary analysis and a short description of the source of the
data. What stays out is a lecture on standard methods: a journal article
names the median instead of explaining it (Döring, 2023, p. 771), and the
results stay out as well.

**Results.** The results report what you found, in the order of the questions
from the introduction, with the numbers and the size of the groups behind
them. Each finding appears exactly once, in the text, in a table or in a
figure, never twice or three times (Döring, 2023, p. 772). What stays out is
every explanation, why something is so and what it means, and words like
"remarkably" or "surprisingly", which already judge.

**Discussion.** After the abstract, the discussion is the part experts read
first (Döring, 2023, p. 770), and it begins with the answer to the question
from the introduction. It then compares this answer with other studies, names
the limitations of the data and the method, and says what follows for
practice and for further research. What stays out are new results, a long
repetition of every number, and a vague call for more research as the only
conclusion.

## Part 3: the most common mistake (frames 19–28)

The mistake that appears most often is not a wrong number but a sentence in
the wrong section.

**Quick quiz** (live poll `paper-sentence-section`). Where does this sentence
belong: "vegetarians named a higher price limit, probably because they value
animal welfare"? As it stands, in the discussion, because its second half
interprets. Its first half, however, is a finding, and findings belong in
the results.

**Result or interpretation.** The sentence holds two statements, and they
belong in two sections. The finding comes from the milk survey: of the people
who said from which price on the milk shown to them would be too expensive,
the 29 vegetarians named a median of 2.49 euros and the 379 omnivores a
median of 1.99 euros. That goes into the results, with its numbers. Why
vegetarians name a higher limit the data do not say; the guess that they
value animal welfare goes into the discussion, marked as a possibility,
compared with what other studies found, and with the remark that 29 people
are a small group.

**Dissecting a real paper.** The outline is that of an article on
plant-based milk by Dorina, Nikpour, Mullan and Uren (2025, *Foods* 14(11),
1970, CC BY 4.0), an online survey of 286 people that follows imrad
throughout. Read every paper in this order: first the question at the end of
the introduction, here section 1.2, "the current study", with its three
hypotheses; then the answer at the start of the discussion, whose first
paragraph says which hypotheses were supported; then the limitations, here
the subsection "strengths and limitations", before trusting the answer. Only
then check whether the methods carry it. Whoever reads the results closely
will also find a number in them that cannot be right.

## Part 4: the abstract (frames 29–35)

**The paper in miniature.** The abstract is the whole paper in one paragraph,
with one sentence for each part (after Döring, 2023, p. 776). For the example
from the milk survey it could read:

- problem: milk competes on price, so it matters where people's price limits
  lie.
- question: do people with different diets name different price limits for
  milk?
- method: we reanalysed an existing survey, in which 611 of 2,809 people
  named a limit.
- findings: the vegetarians' median limit was 2.49 euros, that of omnivores
  1.99 euros.
- conclusion: price limits may differ by diet, which a larger study should
  test.

It is written last, when the findings are known, and journals usually allow
between 150 and 250 words; the APA Publication Manual (2020) sets 250 as the
upper limit.

**Closing.** The floor plan from the beginning now has its house. Write your
question at the end of the introduction and your answer at the start of the
discussion, and let everything else connect the two. With this structure you
can set up the template of your own paper: the headings are already there,
and you fill the rooms.
