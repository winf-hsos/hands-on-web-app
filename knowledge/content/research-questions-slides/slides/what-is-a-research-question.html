First the way from a topic to a question you can answer.

topic, problem, question

after Döring (2023), Forschungsmethoden und Evaluation, p. 148
A topic only names a subject, here plant-based milk. The research problem says which insight you want to gain about which aspect of it, here how the price affects whether people choose oat milk over cow's milk. The question turns this into something that data can answer: a product, a measured outcome and a change in price. The most common mistake of beginners is to stop at the topic, because a topic sounds like a plan but tells you nothing about what to compute.

two conditions

after Huntington-Klein, The Effect, ch. 2
Huntington-Klein asks two things of a research question: there must be evidence that can answer it, and the answer must tell us something about how the world works, for instance why people do what they do. "Which milk ad is the best?" fails the first condition, because nothing says what "best" means, so no evidence can decide it. "How many respondents live in Lower Saxony?" passes the first and fails the second: the column q003land answers it, but the number teaches us nothing about behaviour. The question in green meets both, and the survey has a column for each of its parts. A useful test is whether an unexpected result would change your mind; if it would not, the question is not yet a research question.

question or hypothesis

after Döring (2023), Forschungsmethoden und Evaluation, pp. 149–152
A research question is open: it asks, often with a w-word, and it fits a field in which there is not yet enough prior knowledge for a firm expectation. A hypothesis is a claim with a direction, and it is not a hunch: it has to rest on theory or on earlier findings, which you cite. Where that prior knowledge is missing, you ask an open question instead. For the analysis of a survey that is already collected, the open question is the normal case, and a hypothesis is only worth stating when you can name where the expectation comes from.
Now what separates a good question from one that only sounds good.

three kinds of questions

What you may claim at the end depends on the kind of question. A descriptive question counts and summarises: 73 percent of the respondents name the discounter as a place where they shop for food. A relational question compares groups or asks whether two things go together: in big cities 9 percent shop for food online, in villages 1 percent. A causal question asks what happens when one thing changes, and it needs a design that separates the cause from everything that goes along with it. Describing and comparing works with any survey, while a cause can only be claimed where the data allow it; in this survey the price experiment for alpro oat drink is such a place. a question you can answer well is worth more than an important one you can only talk about.
This is the sentence of the second part. An important question that the data cannot reach ends in speculation, while a modest question answered well adds a fact.
Some questions sound like research questions and still cannot be answered with these data.

four traps

These questions are not stupid, but these data cannot answer them. A normative question asks what should be, and data only describe what is. A vague question has no object and no measure. A causal question without a design asks for a cause where the survey can only show that things go together: people with a higher income also differ in education, in where they live and in their values, and one survey cannot tell these apart. And a question about change needs more than one point in time, while this survey was asked once, in November 2025. The normative question can be turned into how many respondents hold a view, the causal one into a question about a relation, and the vague one can be narrowed down.

rescue the question

“makes” becomes “is related to”, and the report says honestly why it cannot claim more.
The rescued question keeps the interest of the original one and asks only what the data can show: whether household income (d049einkommen) and the share of organic food (v032bioanteil) go together. In the report you then say plainly that a relation is not yet a cause, and why these data cannot separate income from what comes with it.

Now the way from a vague idea to a question in which every word points into the data.

from idea to question

The idea "prices and oat milk" becomes a question once six fields are filled: who was asked, where and when, what varies, what is measured and what is compared with what. Whoever can fill all six has a question, and whoever gets stuck at one field now knows where the question is still vague. The people are those who bought and drank milk or milk alternatives in the last twelve months, the panel ran online across Germany in November 2025, and the price of alpro varied between four levels.
the sharpened question
how does purchase certainty for alpro oat drink change among people who buy and drink milk when its price lies 10 cents below, or 10 or 30 cents above, the regular 1.79 euros?
This is a question you can put in the title of a report, and every word in it corresponds to a column or to a row filter in the data set, as the codebook will show.

the price experiment

chance made the groups alike except for the price, so a difference in purchase certainty can be put down to it.
Every respondent in this test saw the same shelf with Weihenstephan at 1.79 euros and Gut & Günstig at 1.09 euros, and alpro at one of four prices: 1.69, 1.79, 1.89 or 2.09 euros. For each of the three milks they said how surely they would buy it, from minus 4 for certainly not to plus 4 for certainly. Because chance decided who saw which price, the groups differ only in the price, and here, and only here, you may say that the price changes purchase certainty. That is the difference between a design you have inherited and a question that only sounds causal.
Finally the test every question has to pass when the data were collected before the question.

the data came first

The data are collected, and no question can create a column afterwards. So the question adapts to the data and not the other way round: adapting the question is the normal way of working, while talking the data into fitting the question is the mistake. If every word of the question has a column, it is answerable; if a part is missing, you adapt the question and check it again; and if nothing fits, you drop it. In applied market research this is everyday work, because most analyses use data that someone else collected for another purpose.
