Many projects start with a topic like this one, and that is fine as a start. But a topic does not say what to compute: the price, the brands, who buys it, why, or how often. The next slide shows the way from the shelf to one question about it.

topic, problem, question

after Döring (2023), Forschungsmethoden und Evaluation, p. 148
A topic only names a subject, here plant-based milk. The research problem says which insight you want to gain about which aspect of it, here how the price affects whether people choose oat milk over cow's milk. The question turns this into something that data can answer: a product, a measured outcome and a change in price. The most common mistake of beginners is to stop at the topic, because a topic sounds like a plan but tells you nothing about what to compute.

two conditions

after Huntington-Klein, The Effect, ch. 2
Huntington-Klein asks two things of a research question: there must be evidence that can answer it, and the answer must tell us something about how the world works, for instance why people do what they do. Think of the two conditions as a map. "Which milk ad is the best?" sounds interesting but fails the first condition, because nothing says what "best" means, so no evidence can decide it. "How many respondents live in a big city?" is answerable with the column D044stadtf, but the number teaches us nothing about behaviour. The question in the top right meets both: age and the share of organic food are both in the survey, and either answer would teach us something. The answer, by the way, runs against what many expect: among respondents under 30, 54 percent buy at least half of their food organic, among those over 60 only 38 percent. A useful test is whether an unexpected result would change your mind; if it would not, the question is not yet a research question.

research question or hypothesis

after Döring (2023), Forschungsmethoden und Evaluation, pp. 149–152
without an expectation you can cite, the open question is the honest choice.
Both sentences are about the same thing, age and the share of organic food, and the difference lies in how much they commit to. The research question is open: it asks whether the share differs by age and expects no direction, which fits a field in which there is not yet enough prior knowledge for a firm expectation. The hypothesis makes the same question concrete: it names two groups, over 60 and under 30, and a direction, larger, so that the data can prove it wrong. That is not a hunch: a hypothesis has to rest on theory or on earlier findings, which you cite. Where that prior knowledge is missing, you ask the open question. Whether the data are already collected does not decide this, but whether you can say, before you look at the data, where the expectation comes from. With a ready-made survey you often cannot, and a hypothesis written after a first look at the data only describes what you have already seen, so it can no longer fail; then the open question is the honest choice. This one, incidentally, would fail in this survey: among respondents under 30, 54 percent buy at least half of their food organic, among those over 60 only 38 percent.
Now what separates a good question from one that only sounds good.

three kinds of questions

What you may claim at the end depends on the kind of question, and each kind has its own kind of answer. A descriptive question counts and summarises: 73 percent of the respondents name the discounter as a place where they shop for food. A relational question compares groups or asks whether two things go together: in big cities 9 percent shop for food online, elsewhere 4 percent. A causal question asks what happens when one thing changes, and it needs a design that separates the cause from everything that goes along with it. Describing and comparing works with any survey, while a cause can only be claimed where the data allow it; in this survey the price experiment for alpro oat drink is such a place, and its answer comes later. Each line is a question you ask of your own question before you start to compute. Would every possible answer teach you something, or only the one you hope for? Do the data contain what you need, and can you answer it in the weeks you have? Can these data separate what you want to separate, for example a price effect from everything else that differs between people? And is it really one question, or three that you would have to answer one after the other?

four traps

These questions are not stupid, but these data cannot answer them. A normative question asks what should be, and data only describe what is. A vague question names no particular attitude: “think about food” could mean taste, price, health or anything else, so there is nothing to measure. A causal question without a design asks for a cause where the survey can only show that things go together. In an experiment chance would decide who gets more income, but in this survey nobody assigned income: people with a higher income also differ in education, in where they live and in their values, and one survey cannot tell these apart. Experiments with income are rare and expensive, for instance studies that pay randomly chosen people a basic income. And a question about change needs more than one point in time, while this survey was asked once, in November 2025. The normative question can be turned into how many respondents hold a view, the causal one into a question about a relation, and the vague one can be narrowed down.

rescue the question

“make” becomes “is related to”, and the report says honestly why it cannot claim more.
One word turns the question into a causal one: make. The rescued question keeps the interest of the original one and asks only what the data can show: whether household income (d049einkommen) and the share of organic food (v032bioanteil) go together. In the report you then say plainly that a relation is not yet a cause, and why these data cannot separate income from what comes with it.

Sharpening takes something away until a point remains. A question is sharpened the same way: you cut away everything you cannot measure until six things are left that the data can say.

from idea to question

The idea "prices and oat milk" becomes a question once six fields are filled: who was asked, where and when, what varies, what is measured and what is compared with what. Whoever can fill all six has a question, and whoever gets stuck at one field now knows where the question is still vague. The people are those who bought and drank milk or milk alternatives in the last twelve months, the panel ran online across Germany in November 2025, and the price of alpro varied between four levels.
the sharpened question
how does purchase certainty for alpro oat drink change among people who buy and drink milk when its price lies 10 cents below, or 10 or 30 cents above, the regular 1.79 euros?
This is a question you can put in the title of a report, and every word in it corresponds to a column or to a row filter in the data set, as the codebook will show.

the price experiment

chance decided which shelf each respondent saw, so a difference in purchase certainty can be put down to the price.
Every respondent in this test saw the same shelf with Weihenstephan at 1.79 euros and Gut & Günstig at 1.09 euros, and alpro at one of four prices: 1.69, 1.79, 1.89 or 2.09 euros. For each of the three milks they said how surely they would buy it, from minus 4 for certainly not to plus 4 for certainly. Because chance decided who saw which price, the groups differ only in the price, and here, and only here, you may say that the price changes purchase certainty. That is the difference between a design you have inherited and a question that only sounds causal.

and the answer

the price hardly moved purchase certainty, and for a good question that is an answer too.
The four means lie between minus 0.3 and plus 0.1 on a scale from minus 4 to plus 4, and the 95 percent intervals of all four overlap: at 2.09 euros purchase certainty is a little lower than at the regular price, but the difference is smaller than the uncertainty. Each group answered with a spread of about 3 points, which is wide compared with the differences between the groups. So within these 40 cents the price did not change much, at least not on this scale and on this shelf. That is exactly what the checklist meant with potential results: a question is good when the answer "no clear effect" teaches you as much as a large effect would.

the data came first

The data are collected, and no question can create a column afterwards. So the question adapts to the data and not the other way round: adapting the question is the normal way of working, while talking the data into fitting the question is the mistake. If every part of the question has a column in the data, it is answerable; if nothing fits, you drop it; and if a part is missing, you adapt the question and check it again. You check this in the codebook, the documentation that lists every column of the data with its question, who was asked and what each code means. In applied market research this is everyday work, because most analyses use data that someone else collected for another purpose.

check it against the codebook

codebook: the documentation of the data, with every column, its question, who was asked and what each code means
Every place in the question that points into the data has a column, and for every column the codebook says who was asked and what the codes mean. The filter is the first thing to check: according to the questionnaire the price test went to people who bought milk, with or without drinking it, but in the data only code 3, bought and drank, occurs among the 601 respondents who saw a price. Of them 596 rated alpro, between 139 and 161 per price. Time and place of the survey are not columns at all; they come from the study documentation. If a part of your question has no column, the question changes, not the data.
