# Lecture notes: keeping time

These notes accompany the stagekit deck "keeping time" and explain its contents for reading afterwards, in the order of the slides. Build-up steps and the segments of an animation count as frames of their own in the player and in the export (58 frames in all), so the references point to frames. The deck was written on 2026-11-23, taking its starting point from the LiFi deck "sampling and synchronization".

The core message: a receiver only sees light and has to find out by itself when the next symbol begins. There are two basic answers, a shared beat that is set again and again with markers, or a change in the light that serves as the beat, and each of them has a price that shows up in the time a file needs.

## Part 1: why fast breaks

**The orchestra (Frame 4).** Every musician in an orchestra can keep time, and yet without the person with the baton they would drift apart within a minute. That is not a lack of skill: small differences in tempo add up, and nobody notices their own error. Two computers that send each other light have exactly this problem.

**Your homework: where did it tip? (Frames 5 and 6).** The teams report at which rate their transmission broke, with which integration time, and how it broke, and the table on the slide can be typed into during the session. How it broke matters most: letters that shift by one after a while point to clocks drifting apart, while garbled characters from the start point to symbols shorter than the measuring window. These are the two causes the next slides show.

**What the receiver sees (Frames 7 to 9).** At the top the sender switches seven symbols of equal length with clear borders. The next step shows what arrives: one continuous band of light without borders, in which two equal colors in a row merge into one long stretch. The last step lays the sender's borders over it, and at the question marks the receiver sees no change at all. How is it to know when the next symbol begins? That question runs through the whole deck.

**One dot stands for many readings (Frames 10 and 11).** The receiver measures many times within one symbol and averages, and the single readings scatter as described in the lesson on signal and noise. On the following slides a single dot stands for this average and sits where the middle of the readings lies.

**Cause one: no two clocks run alike (Frames 12 to 14).** The top row shows the time slots of the sender, the bottom row the readings of the receiver. Both have agreed on the same rate, but the receiver's clock runs slightly slow, greatly exaggerated here at eight per cent so that the effect shows after a few symbols. The first readings sit almost in the middle of their slot, then they wander further right with every symbol until they land in the neighboring slot, and from there on the receiver reads the wrong symbol, marked in red. The error per symbol is harmless while its sum is not, and since the error grows with time while the slot shrinks with the rate, the problem depends on the speed.

**Cause two: the sensor needs time to look (Frames 15 to 17).** A sensor does not deliver an instantaneous value but collects light over its measuring window and reports the average, its integration time, here 50 ms. On the left the symbols last 100 ms, longer than the window, so the reading lies entirely within one symbol and the value fits. On the right they last 40 ms, shorter than the window, so every reading contains a change, and the sensor reports a mixture that matches no symbol. There is no error message, only a wrong number. A shorter window helps against the mixing but raises the scatter, which is the same trade as with noise.

**One question (Frames 18 to 20).** Both causes are versions of the same question: when does the next symbol begin? The sender knows, because it switches itself, while the receiver only sees light. There are two basic answers, either time tells the receiver or a change does, and the next two parts read out the price tag of each.

## Part 2: a shared beat

**When does it tip? (Frames 22 to 24).** The line is the accumulated clock error over time for two clocks that differ by half a per cent, five milliseconds per second, a straight line that keeps growing. The dashed line is the threshold of half a symbol, 50 ms at ten symbols per second. They cross after ten seconds, and from there on the receiver measures in the neighboring symbol; the check is one line, since 0.5 % of 10 seconds is 50 ms. Half a symbol is enough to fail, because the reading sits in the middle of its slot and has only half the width to spare on either side.

**Faster breaks sooner (Frames 25 and 26).** The same calculation for different rates: half a symbol is 500 ms wide at one symbol per second and only 25 ms at twenty, while the error always grows at the same pace. The same clocks therefore last a hundred seconds at a slow rate and five at a fast one. That explains why a link that runs stably at a slow rate suddenly collapses when it is sped up, although nothing about the clocks has changed.

**Markers reset the error (Frames 27 and 28).** Agreeing once is not enough, while agreeing again and again is: at fixed intervals the sender inserts a marker, and the receiver realigns its time grid to it. The error line becomes a sawtooth that grows, drops to zero at each marker and grows again, so the error can never become larger than what accumulates between two markers, here 20 ms with a marker every four seconds. A marker is a reserved symbol, for instance a color that does not occur in the data alphabet or a short dark pause, and the only requirement is that the receiver cannot confuse it with a data symbol.

**How the receiver uses a marker (Frames 29 to 31).** Seeing the marker in its grid does not help the receiver by itself. It measures faster than symbols arrive, here five times per symbol, and notes when the marker begins: the first reading inside the pause turns yellow. It restarts its grid there, so the next symbol border is the start of the marker plus one symbol length. The grid was clearly off before, and afterwards the error is at most one measuring step. The way with a beat borrows the idea of the other way here, reacting to a change, but only once per block instead of at every symbol.

**Try it: the drift simulator (Frame 32).** In the embedded simulator the receiver's clock runs 2 % slow. Left alone, it shows after how many symbols the readings are half a slot off and when the first red rings, the wrong reads, appear, which is the calculation from before made visible. Setting the slider "sync marker every" to eight symbols keeps the error small, and the display "marker overhead" shows what that costs.

**The price of markers (Frame 33).** Starting together once costs no symbol at all but lets the error grow without limit. Markers limit the error but carry no data, so a marker every eight symbols costs an eighth of the rate. How often a marker is needed depends on how far your own clocks drift apart, and that is measured rather than believed.

**Markers in real links (Frames 34 to 36).** Three real links work with recurring markers. A serial port (UART, RS-232) rests at 1, and every character begins with a start bit 0: at its falling edge the receiver restarts its clock, reads each bit in its middle and expects a stop bit at the end. Since the marker comes before every character, the clocks only have to stay together for about ten bits, and a deviation of a few per cent is enough for that. The time signal transmitter DCF77 near Frankfurt lowers its amplitude briefly at the start of every second, for 100 ms for a 0 and 200 ms for a 1; in second 59 the pulse is missing, and the gap marks the start of the next minute. A VGA monitor gets a pulse on a line of its own after every row of the picture (horizontal sync) and another after every frame (vertical sync), about 31,500 row markers per second at 640 × 480 pixels and 60 frames per second.

## Part 3: the change as the beat

**A change means "next" (Frames 38 and 39).** The naive idea makes every color a symbol and every change of color mean "next, please". The receiver no longer needs a clock; it measures as often as it can and reacts only to changes. But two equal symbols in a row produce no change: six symbols are sent and only three changes are seen.

**The change carries the bit (Frames 40 to 43).** The fix is to let the change carry the information instead of the color. From every color two successors are allowed, one for 1 and one for 0, and the color itself is forbidden as its own successor, so there is always a change to see. On a triangle of three colors a clockwise step means 1 and an anticlockwise one 0: red to green is clockwise, so 1, green back to red is anticlockwise, so 0, and red to blue is anticlockwise as well, so 0. The receiver measures as often as it likes and decodes only at a change.

**What the change costs in bits (Frames 44 and 45).** The freedom from the clock is paid for in capacity. As states, k colors carry log₂ k bits, as changes only log₂(k − 1), because the current color drops out as a successor. Three colors carry 1 bit instead of 1.58, four carry 1.58 instead of 2. Whoever thinks in powers of two falls into a trap here: for a clean two bits per change you need five colors, not four, and in general an alphabet of size 2ⁿ + 1.

**The change as the beat in real links (Frames 46 and 47).** The Manchester code puts a change in the middle of every bit; in 10BASE-T ethernet a change from low to high means 1 and one from high to low 0. The receiver takes its clock from these changes and pays for it, since 10 Mbit/s need up to 20 million changes per second on the line. Infrared remote controls following RC-5 use the same code, with light as on our link and bits of about 1.8 ms. USB 1.x and 2.0 use NRZI, where a 0 is a change and a 1 none. A long run of ones would then have no change, so after six ones the sender inserts a 0 that the receiver removes again, called bit stuffing, which costs at most a seventh of the rate, much like a marker.

**Without a clock is not without time (Frames 48 to 50).** The most common fallacy is that time no longer matters once the clock is gone. Two decisions about time remain. During a change the sensor measures mixed values, as shown in part 1, so the receiver needs a threshold for how long a color must hold still before it counts as a new state; otherwise it invents symbols from the in-between values. And how many changes per second the link manages still decides the throughput, with exactly the limits from part 1.

## Part 4: choosing a way

**Two ways, two price tags (Frames 52 and 53).** With a beat the receiver counts time, markers limit the drift, and each marker is a symbol without data, while every color carries the full log₂ k bits. Without a beat the receiver waits for the change, there is no clock that could drift, but a color has to hold still briefly before it counts and carries only log₂(k − 1) bits. Neither way is right or wrong, and which one fits better depends on your own link.

**What a file costs (Frames 54 and 55).** Whatever the strategy, in the end this calculation counts: the time t for a file is the number of bits n divided by symbols per second r times bits per symbol b. It has exactly two levers, b and r, and everything in this deck decides about both.

**2 KiB at five symbols per second (Frames 56 and 57).** 2 KiB are 16,384 bits. With a beat, four colors carry 2 bits per symbol, which at five symbols per second gives 10 bit/s and a little over 27 minutes, not counting the markers. Without a beat the same four colors carry only 1.58 bits per change, which makes about 34 minutes. That is the price of the freedom from the clock, in minutes. How far the two levers can be turned at all is a question of its own, that of throughput and its limits.

**Closing (Frame 58).** No two clocks run alike, which is not a quirk of a particular laptop but a property of the world. The only choice is what you do about it: agree again and again with markers, or make the change itself the beat. Which way pays off is shown by a measurement on your own link.
