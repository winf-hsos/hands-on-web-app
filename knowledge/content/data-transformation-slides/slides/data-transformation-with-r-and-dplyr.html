This input is about data transformation: turning the table you have into the table that answers your question. The message to take home is that a handful of verbs is enough for almost every data preparation, and that the pipe chains them into a sequence you can read like a sentence.

A box of building bricks, all mixed up. Nobody builds straight out of this box: first you pick out the bricks you need, sort them, group them by colour or size, and only then do you put something together. Data works the same way. Hardly any question can be read off the raw data, but a handful of basic operations, such as picking, sorting, grouping and summarizing, turns any table into the one that holds the answer.
First the idea, without any code: what transforming data means and which basic operations there are.

a question for the survey

the answer is in the table, but you cannot read it off.
This is our survey on milk and organic food: 30 rows, one per respondent, and eleven columns. The question is whether vegetarians buy more organic food than others. Everything we need is in two columns, diet and organic_share. But the answer only appears once we take the right rows, split them by diet and compute an average per group. That is data transformation: making one table out of another, without collecting new data.

a table in, a table out

every transformation takes a table and returns a table, which is why they can be chained.
First key: a table goes into a transformation. Note that the table itself stays where it is; the transformation works on a copy, and the original is never changed. Second key: what comes out is again a table, usually a smaller one. Third key: because it is a table, it can go into the next transformation, which again returns a table. Fourth key: and so on, as often as the question needs, and in data analysis the chain often ends in a chart. This is the one property everything else in this input rests on.

six basic operations

Six basic operations cover almost everything, and each answers a question. Key by key, each one acts on a copy of a small table. Selecting columns, which columns do I need: two columns go. Filtering rows, who is this about: two rows go. Sorting, what comes first: the rows take the order of their numbers. Computing new columns, what can be derived from what is there: a new column is filled. Summarizing, what is typical and how many are there: many rows become one. Grouping, how do subgroups differ: every row is marked with its group, and nothing else changes. Grouping is the odd one out, because on its own it changes nothing; it makes the next operation work per group. Last key: in dplyr each operation is one verb, and these are their names.

The six operations are not an invention of R. SQL has them, pandas in Python has them, and in Excel you do them by hand with filters, sorting and pivot tables. Whoever has understood the operations once finds their way around SQL and pandas quickly, because only the names change.
Now the same operations in R, with the package dplyr, which comes with the tidyverse. We take one verb at a time and watch on six rows what it does.

dplyr names its functions after what they do

library(tidyverse)
We load dplyr with the tidyverse, which brings the other packages we use along with it. dplyr calls its functions verbs, because each is named after what it does: select, filter, arrange, mutate, summarize. Three rules hold for all of them, shown here on filter(). First key: the first argument is always a table, here the survey. Second key: columns go by their bare name, without quotes and without the dollar sign. Third key: what comes out is a new table, and the survey stays as it was. That last rule often surprises people: if you want to keep the result, you have to assign it to a name. Fourth key: the same call, written the way we will always write it. The table moves in front of the verb, and the pipe passes it into the first slot. Read it as: take the survey, and then filter. Part 3 shows why this is better.

six rows of the survey

organic_share: share of organic food when shopping, from 1 (hardly any) to 5 (almost all)
To watch the verbs at work we take the first six rows and four columns of the survey: an id, the age, the diet and the share of organic food when shopping, from 1 for hardly any to 5 for almost all. The table stays exactly here on the next slides, so you can see what each verb changes.

select(): keep columns

survey |> select(id, diet)
As announced, we write every verb with the pipe: survey, and then select. select() keeps the columns you name. First key: we name id and diet. Second key: the other two columns disappear and diet moves up to id. All six rows are still there; select() never touches the rows.

different ways to say which columns

survey |> select(2:5)

survey |> select(id:diet)

survey |> select(starts_with("organic"))

survey |> select(where(is.numeric))

survey |> select(id, starts_with("organic"))

more helpers, such as ends_with() and contains(), in the help: ?select
There are many ways to say which columns you want, and here are the ones that come up most. By name, as before. By position: 2:5 takes the second to the fifth column. A range of names with a colon: every column from id to diet, in the order of the table. With a minus sign: all columns except this one. By a pattern in the name: starts_with() takes every column whose name begins with organic. By type: where(is.numeric) takes every column that holds numbers. And the ways combine: id by name plus every organic column by pattern. The help page of select() lists more helpers. With eleven columns this saves little; with two hundred it saves a lot.

filter(): keep rows

survey |> filter(age >= 50)
filter() keeps the rows for which a condition is true. First key: R checks the condition row by row, is the age at least 50? Yes, yes, no, no, yes, no. Second key: the rows with a no disappear, and three rows remain. The columns stay as they were; filter() never touches the columns.

conditions

comparisons and logical operators
= assigns, == compares
A condition is a comparison. Two equals signs ask whether two things are equal; one equals sign would assign, and R complains. The ampersand combines two conditions that must both hold, the vertical bar two of which one is enough. %in% asks whether a value is one of several, and is.na() asks whether a value is missing; the exclamation mark turns it round, so the last pipeline keeps every row that has an organic share.

arrange(): sort rows

survey |> arrange(age)
arrange() sorts the rows by the column you name. First key: by age, and without anything else the order is ascending, from the youngest to the oldest: 32, 47, 48, 55, 56, 74. Second key: desc() around the column turns the order round, from the oldest to the youngest. Nothing is added or removed, only the order changes.

ascending, descending, several columns

survey |> arrange(age)
ascending, from the youngest
survey |> arrange(desc(age))
descending, from the oldest
survey |> arrange(-age)
also descending, a shortcut for numbers
survey |> arrange(diet, desc(age))
by diet, within it from the oldest
Without desc() arrange() sorts in ascending order, and desc() turns it round. For numbers a minus sign does the same as desc() and is quicker to type; for text it does not work, there you need desc(). With several columns the second one only decides where the first one is tied: here the rows are sorted by diet, and within each diet from the oldest to the youngest.

mutate(): compute new columns

survey |> mutate(decade = age %/% 10 * 10)
mutate() adds a column computed from the others. %/% is integer division, so age %/% 10 * 10 turns 56 into 50: the decade. First key: the new column appears. Second key: it is filled row by row, each value from the age in the same row. The number of rows stays the same.

one row in, one row out

survey |> mutate( decade = age %/% 10 * 10, organic_fan = organic_share >= 4 )
mutate() computes in every row on its own, one row in and one row out. It can create several columns at once, and a column may use one computed earlier in the same call. The second column here is a logical value: TRUE for everyone who buys mostly organic food.

summarize(): many rows become one

survey |> summarize(n = n(), mean_organic = mean(organic_share))
summarize() turns many rows into one. First key: n() counts the rows, one to six. Second key: mean() takes the six organic shares, 2, 2, 1, 1, 2 and 2, and turns them into one number, 1.67. What remains is a table with a single row and only the columns we computed.

frequently used summary functions

survey |> summarize( n = n(), mean_organic = mean(organic_share, na.rm = TRUE), oldest = max(age) )

Inside summarize() you use functions that turn many values into one. These six come up most often: n(), mean(), median(), min(), max(), sd(); there are many more, such as quantile() or n_distinct(), and any function that turns many values into one will do. One warning: a single missing value makes the mean missing as well, because R does not know what the missing value would have been. na.rm = TRUE tells it to leave missing values out.

group_by(): split into groups

survey |> group_by(diet)
group_by() splits the table into groups. First key: every row gets the colour of its diet, and R notes above the table that it is grouped by diet into three groups. Look closely: the data have not changed, not a single value. Second key: we pull the groups apart so you can see them. R keeps the rows where they are; the grouping only matters to the next verb.

group_by() and summarize(): one row per group

survey |> group_by(diet) |> summarize(n = n(), mean_age = mean(age))
Two verbs in a row: group by diet, and then summarize. group_by() alone changes nothing you can see; it tells the next verb to work per group. On the key, every group collapses into one row with its count and its mean age: one flexitarian aged 74, four omnivores with a mean of 47.5, one vegetarian aged 48. The groups come out in alphabetical order.

Quick check. Respondents are rows, and keeping some rows is filter(), with the condition state == "Bayern". select() would be the answer for columns.
you need the average age per diet. what do you use?
  1. summarize()
  2. group_by()
  3. group_by() |> summarize()
  4. mutate()
Per diet means groups, and an average means summarize(). summarize() alone gives one average for everybody, group_by() alone changes nothing visible; it takes both.
We have used the pipe since the first verb. Now we look at what R code looks like without it, and at what the pipe does.

nested calls are read from the inside out

arrange( summarize( group_by( filter(survey, age >= 40), diet ), n = n() ), desc(n) )
Four verbs: keep everyone aged 40 or older, group by diet, count, sort by the count. Without the pipe, every verb takes the table as its first argument, so the verbs end up inside each other. Nested, R runs them from the inside out. Key by key: first filter, then group_by, then summarize, and arrange last, although it stands first. And the arguments stand far from their function: desc(n) at the very bottom belongs to arrange() at the very top.

intermediate tables

older <- filter(survey, age >= 40) grouped <- group_by(older, diet) counts <- summarize(grouped, n = n()) result <- arrange(counts, desc(n))
readable, but full of names nobody needs again.
The other way is to give every step a name. That reads from top to bottom, but now we have four tables in our environment, three of which nobody needs again, and every name is a chance to pick the wrong one.

the pipe passes the table on

The pipe, a vertical bar followed by a greater-than sign, takes what stands on its left and puts it into the first slot of the function on its right. On the key, x travels into that slot: x |> f(y) is the same as f(x, y). This is why the first-argument rule of dplyr matters: every verb takes a table first, so a table can be piped into every verb. Next key: read the pipe as "and then". In older code you will see %>%, which does the same. Last key: ctrl+shift+m types the pipe in RStudio and Positron, cmd+shift+m on a Mac.

a pipeline

The same four steps as a pipeline, station by station, and under each station the table it returns. First key: filter() drops the 32-year-old, five rows remain. Second key: group_by() gives the rows the colours of their groups and changes nothing else. Third key: summarize() makes one row per group with its count, three omnivores, one flexitarian and one vegetarian, each in the colour of its group. Fourth key: arrange() sorts by the count, so the omnivores come first. Each table goes on to the next station as a copy, so every intermediate result stays in view.

the same, as you would write it

survey |> filter(age >= 40) |> group_by(diet) |> summarize(n = n()) |> arrange(desc(n))
take the survey,
and then keep everyone aged 40 or older,
and then group by diet,
and then count,
and then sort by the count.
This is how you write it: one step per line, the pipe at the end of each line. And this is how you read it, key by key: take the survey, and then keep everyone aged 40 or older, and then group by diet, and then count, and then sort by the count. The code reads in the order in which it runs.

the real task is breaking the question into steps, because translating each step into a verb is the easy part.
Back to our question: do vegetarians buy more organic food than others? Before writing code, plan the steps in words. Take the survey. Keep the rows that have an organic share. Split by diet. Compute an average and a count per group. Sort by the average. Then translate line by line, key by key; each line becomes one verb. Last key: this is the actual work. Knowing the verbs is quickly learned; the thinking lies in splitting a question into steps in the right order, and that is what you practise in every exercise.

six verbs and a pipe

Six verbs and a pipe. With these you can prepare almost any table: choose columns and rows, sort, compute, summarize, per group if needed, and chain it all with "and then".
